Drone Day Rate vs. Project Price: What Each One Actually Buys Your Production

You hold two numbers for the same aerial work and they do not describe the same purchase. One buys time on a schedule. The other buys a finished outcome. Choosing the wrong one is how a production pays twice, once for the day and once to fix it in post.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Producers compare aerial quotes the way they compare any crew line, and that instinct is sound until the structures differ. A day rate and a project price can both be honest numbers for the same location and still buy very different things. The day rate buys a professional unit, on your call sheet, for a defined block, delivering files for your editor. The project price buys a defined result, such as a finished aerial sequence, with planning and edit folded into one figure. Neither is better in the abstract. Each fits a different kind of risk.

What a day rate actually buys

A day rate buys presence, judgment, and delivery discipline inside your production. The unit arrives briefed, checks the location and airspace in advance, flies in sync with your schedule, logs takes, and hands over organised files matched to your settings. You keep creative control. Your director directs. Your editor edits. The aerial unit behaves like any other department for that block of time, and unused creative decisions remain yours to make on the day.

The published picture in this region is plain. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day. National production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 per day, and higher for larger productions. Those are the numbers a producer can hold against any quote, including ours. Our own written quotes list deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and production files are delivered within two business days.

  • Best fit. Commercials and brand films where your team owns the edit.
  • You control. Shot order, extra takes, and how the aerial sits in the cut.
  • You still carry. The edit, the colour finish, and any extra locations beyond the block.
  • Watch for. Quotes that omit planning, file organisation, or delivery timing.

What a project price actually buys

A project price buys an outcome with the edges defined in advance. The quote names the shots or the finished sequence, the locations, the deliverables, and the date. Planning, flying, selects, and in some cases the aerial edit are inside one number. That structure suits a production that needs a known result for a client approval, a fixed bid, or a delivery where no one on the core team has time to supervise the aerial edit.

It also suits work centred in one place, such as a venue film near the Old Market or a campus sequence in Papillion, where the flight path can be designed tightly and the outcome described before the day. The trade is flexibility. If the client changes location, adds a second site across the metro, or extends the concept after the price is set, the scope changes and the price should be revisited in writing. A good project quote says what is not included as clearly as what is.

How to choose for this production

Choose a day rate when the schedule is the scarce thing. If talent, light, and a location window in Omaha must align on one day, and your editor is already booked, buy the block and direct it. Choose a project price when the deliverable is the scarce thing. If a client has approved a specific film and you need that film, not a folder of options, buy the outcome and hold the quote to it.

Ask three questions of either quote before you sign. What happens if weather closes the flight window? What exactly arrives, in what form, and by what date? What usage rights are included for the channels this work will run in? On our work, weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer, the location and airspace check is free, and replies come within one business day. Those terms should be visible in writing, whoever you hire.

The honest comparison most quotes avoid

A low day rate is not a saving if it leaves your editor with unmatched files, no log, and a second day of sorting. A high project price is not value if it only describes the same half day of flying with an edit you did not need. We will say this even when it points away from us. If your production only needs two simple establishing shots and your own crew can supervise a local operator for an hour of flight inside a larger day, a full project price from us is more than the job requires.

Equally, if the aerial carries the opening of a commercial and the client will judge the whole film by it, the cheapest day rate in the market is the expensive choice when it fails. Price the risk, not just the line. Put both structures against the same brief, the same deliverables, and the same delivery date. When the descriptions finally match, the right number usually becomes obvious.
