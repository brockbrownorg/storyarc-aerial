How to Brief an Aerial Unit (So Your Editor Gets Footage That Cuts)

The fear in every edit bay is the same. The aerial drive arrives, the clips look fine on their own, and none of them cut against the ground coverage. A clear brief prevents that day, and it takes less from you than most producers expect.

August 2026

Your editor does not need more aerial footage. Your editor needs the right aerial footage, shot with an entry, a reveal, and a clean way out, at the frame rate and in the profile your timeline already uses. When that does not happen, the problem is rarely the flying. It is the brief. A strong brief tells the aerial unit what job each shot does in the story, where it sits in the sequence, and what the ground unit is already covering, so the air work fills gaps instead of repeating them.

That is also why the brief matters more than the aircraft. A short note that says we need a hero shot of the building will produce a pleasant orbit. A brief that says we open on the street, rise over the roofline, and land the eye on the entrance where talent enters in the next ground shot will produce a sequence. The second version costs you ten extra minutes before the shoot and saves hours in post.

Start with the sequence, not the shot list alone

Share the boards, the script pages, or even a rough cut if you have one. State where the aerial sits. Is it an opening that sets place, a transition between scenes, a product reveal, or a closing image? Each purpose flies differently. An opener can be slower and wider. A transition needs a clear direction of travel so the cut feels motivated. A reveal needs restraint early and precision at the end point.

Include the ground coverage plan in plain terms. Tell the unit what lenses and movement the main camera is using, what talent action happens below, and which lines must remain clean for graphics or titles. If a lower third will sit over the lower part of frame, or a client logo cannot be crossed at speed, say so now. Editors forgive many things. They rarely forgive learning about a title safe problem after wrap.

  • Purpose per shot. Opener, transition, reveal, or closer, in one line each.
  • Entry and out point. Where the move starts, where it ends, and what the next shot is.
  • Ground match. Frame rate, profile, and any test frame you want checked on the day.
  • Must avoid list. Signs, people, vehicles, or areas the client does not want shown.

What belongs on the call sheet handover

Send the call sheet, the location address or pin, call time, wrap target, and one named contact who can make decisions on set. Add parking and load in notes, the talent schedule for any moment the aerial must sync with, and any venue contact who controls doors, elevators, or roof access. In Omaha, a downtown location near the RiverFront or the Old Market can look simple on a map and still need venue permission and an airspace check because Eppley Airfield shapes what flies near the centre of the city. That check is part of planning, not a surprise for shoot morning.

Our process for this sits on our page for filmmakers, and it is built around one point of contact and written notes back to you. When you send a call sheet, expect flight feasibility notes within one business day, including any shot we would design differently and why. If a location needs more lead time for permission, you hear that before you lock the schedule, not after crew is booked.

Agree the files before anyone flies

State the delivery shape in the brief. Do you want all raw clips, curated selects, or a finished aerial sequence? What naming pattern does your editor use? Where do files go, and who confirms receipt? StoryArc delivers production files within two business days as standard, organised and labelled, with a short flight log that notes locations, times, and standout takes. If your edit needs selects sooner, say so in the brief and the day can be built around that need.

Also agree usage in writing. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. That line protects your client as much as it protects us, because a commercial, a paid social cut, and an internal film do not carry the same rights needs. Naming them early avoids a difficult conversation when the client asks for one more version in a new channel.

Pricing clarity helps the brief too. As our guide to day rate and project price explains, a day block buys scheduled time and delivery for post, while a project price buys a defined outcome. Tell the unit which structure you are buying under, so expectations about extra takes, extra locations, and edit help stay aligned.

The honest limits to write into the brief

Here is the part that costs us something to say plainly. Some shots should not be briefed as aerial at all. A small interior with low ceilings and a dialogue scene under it will usually read better from a ground move. A tight product detail belongs to the main unit. If your brief includes a shot we believe will not cut or cannot be flown as drawn at that location, we will flag it and propose the version that will work. That may mean a different line, a different time of day, or no flight for that beat.

Nebraska wind is the other honest limit. A brief can name a preferred light window, and it should, but it also needs a backup order of shots so the calmest part of the day is used for the hardest move. Weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer on our work, and building the backup order into the brief is how your day keeps moving even if the first plan changes. Brief the outcome you need, the order you prefer, and the fallback you accept. Do that, and your editor opens a drive that feels like it was shot by someone who had already seen the film.
