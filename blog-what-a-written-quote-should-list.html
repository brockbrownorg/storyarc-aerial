What a Written Quote Should List (and What a Handshake Quote Hides)

A handshake quote feels friendly and fast. It also leaves every important question to memory, and memory is at its worst exactly when you need it most: on a windy shoot day, against a deadline, with money already spent. A written quote is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a disagreement and a document.

December 2025

The lines every quote should carry

  • The deliverables, named. Which films, how many, what lengths, what formats, and whether social cutdowns are included.
  • The delivery date. Not a mood, a date, plus what happens if it slips.
  • Usage rights. Where the film may run, for how long, and who may use it.
  • The location and the time on site. The address the price is based on, and the hours or day block included.
  • Planning and permissions. Who completes the airspace and location check, and who secures venue permission.
  • Revisions. How many rounds are included and what further rounds cost.
  • Weather terms. Who makes the call, when, and whether moving the day costs anything.
  • What is excluded. Raw files, rush delivery, extra locations, extra versions, or travel, named as exclusions rather than discovered as fees.
  • The total, and what would change it. The price for this scope, and the specific additions that would move it.

That is the full shape. Our written quotes list deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights as a matter of standard, and the rest of the lines above travel with them. What a handshake quote hides, without meaning to

Most handshake quotes are not dishonest. They are incomplete in the specific places where projects actually strain. Nobody shook hands on a lie about revisions. The subject simply never came up, so the buyer assumed two rounds and the operator assumed none, and both people are surprised later. The same pattern repeats with usage, where the buyer assumes ads are fine and the operator assumed web only, and with weather, where the buyer assumes a free move and the operator assumes a rebooking fee.

Omaha adds its own version of this. A verbal price given without an address may quietly assume an easy location. When the real address turns out to be downtown near Eppley Airfield, or at a RiverFront venue with its own permission process, the planning work grows and the friendly number starts to wobble. A written quote forces the address into the conversation while changing it is still free.

Time hides the same way. Event coverage sold as being there for the day can mean four hours to one person and ten to another. During College World Series weeks, when an event day runs long and the city runs full, that difference is worth real money and real fatigue. In writing, a half day or a full day block has edges both sides can point to.

How to ask for a written quote without friction

Keep it simple and make it normal. Say that your business books work on written quotes, and ask the operator to put the scope, deliverables, date, usage, and total in one document. A professional will not be offended. Putting things in writing is how professionals prefer to work, because the document protects them as much as it protects you. Hesitation at this request is the single clearest early warning a buyer gets, and it arrives while walking away is still easy.

Once you have it, read the exclusions line twice. That line is where future invoices live. If raw files matter to you, if a second location is possible, if rush delivery might be needed, get each priced as an option now. An option priced in advance is a choice. The same item requested later is a negotiation, held at the moment of your weakest leverage.

Our honesty line: a written quote can also expose that our price is simply higher than another complete quote for the same stated job. That happens, and when the other document is genuinely complete, you should take it. We would rather compete against paper than win against a handshake and inherit its confusion.

Ask for your quote in writing through the quote page, and keep the document, even if the shoot is months away. When the day comes, everyone on it should be working from the same page, literally.

There is also a fairness point worth keeping. A written quote does not exist to trap an operator in amber. Projects genuinely change: a second location becomes necessary, an event grows, a deliverable earns a second version. A good document handles change the way it handles everything else, by naming the change, pricing it, and agreeing it before the extra work happens. What the document prevents is not change. It prevents silent change, where the scope grows in conversation and the price grows in the invoice, and the distance between those two moments is filled with assumption. Keep the paper current and it stays a protection for everyone at the table.

Keep one copy with your business records and one with whoever will manage the film after it arrives. The second copy matters more often than people expect, because the person holding the usage question a year later is rarely the person who signed the quote on the day.
