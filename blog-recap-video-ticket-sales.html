Your Recap Video Is Next Year's Ticket Sales Team

Your event ended, the tents came down, and now the hard part starts again. You need next year to be bigger, and you need people who were not there to believe it is worth their money and their Saturday.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The buyer you are really filming for

The most valuable viewer of your recap film never attended. She saw a friend share a clip, she wondered what she missed, and she decided in about thirty seconds whether next year goes on her calendar. The person who was there already knows it was good. The person who stayed home needs to feel the scale, the crowd energy at the edges of the grounds, the light at the end of the day, and the moment the whole place lifted at once.

That is why a recap planned only as a souvenir misses the point. Planned as a sales tool, it answers three questions for a stranger. What does it feel like to be there. How big and well run is this. Will I regret missing it again. If your film answers those, ticket sales stop depending only on memory and word of mouth.

There is an honest limit here, and it matters. A recap film cannot save an event people did not enjoy, and it cannot fix a weak lineup, bad parking, or a ticket page that confuses buyers. What it can do is give a good event the proof it deserves, in a form people can share in seconds.

What a sales ready recap actually contains

Think in layers, not in one long highlight reel that only your board will watch to the end.

  • The arrival and the scale. Grounds filling, gates opening, the site seen whole from the air so a stranger grasps the size at once.
  • The peak moments, kept short. The headline set, the winning play, the ceremony, the fireworks if you have them. Short enough that the energy never sags.
  • Real faces at a respectful distance. Crowds filmed from the perimeter and from above open ground, never by flying over guests. Joy reads on camera without putting a drone above a crowd.
  • The details sponsors paid for. Banners, stages, and funded features shown working in context, not pasted in as logos at the end.
  • A clear end. Next year dates if you have them, or a simple invitation to follow along until you do.

From that same material you should also get short vertical cuts for social, a sponsor version, and a clean master you can reuse when grant reports and renewal talks come around. One day of filming should feed a full year of asking.

Why the air changes the sale

Ground cameras are good at faces and stages. They struggle to show scale, and scale is what sells a stranger. An aerial opening over an Omaha festival ground, with the downtown skyline beyond it and the Missouri River catching the evening light, tells a viewer this is a real event in a real place before a single word is spoken. Around the RiverFront and the Old Market, that sense of place is part of the product, and only the air shows it whole.

The flight work has to respect the crowd. Our practice is simple and it is stated in writing before the day. We launch and land from clear ground away from guests, we film crowds from the edges and at a distance, and we use height and lens rather than position over people to get the wide shot. If a shot would require flying over your guests, we find another way to tell that part of the story or we leave it out.

Plan the recap before the gates open

The films that sell next year were decided before this year started. Sit down while planning is still cheap and agree on the moments that cannot be missed, the sponsor obligations that must appear, the places a camera and a drone can and cannot go, and the weather call that protects an immovable date. Nebraska wind and a fast summer storm can close a flying window in an hour, so the plan needs a ground level fallback for every aerial moment you care about most.

Agree on delivery while you are at it. Finished films within five business days is our stated standard, and your written quote should list deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights for every cut. If a vendor will not put those three things in writing, the low price is carrying hidden risk.

Cost is part of that early talk too. Our guide to what event aerial coverage costs lays out the half day and full day framing before you commit, so the recap can be budgeted as the sales asset it is rather than as an afterthought bought with what is left.

When a recap is not worth buying

If your event sells out every year with a waiting list, a polished recap is a nice extra, not a need. Spend on the attendee experience first. If your audience never watches video where you plan to post it, say a private gathering with no public sale next year, the math also weakens. We would rather tell you that in the first conversation than sell you a film with nowhere to work.

For most public events in the metro, from Papillion days to Bellevue gatherings to a full weekend in downtown Omaha, the recap is the one purchase from this year that keeps working until the next gates open. Treat it that way in the plan, protect the moments that sell, and next year starts with proof instead of hope.
