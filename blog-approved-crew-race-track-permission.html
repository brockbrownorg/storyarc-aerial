Approved Crew: How Drone Coverage Actually Gets Permission at a Race Track

You want aerial coverage of your race day, but the track rule book says no drones, and you are not sure if that means no for everyone or no until someone does it properly. It almost always means the second one, and the difference matters for your event, your crowd, and your name on the weekend.

June 2026

The default answer at a track is no, and that is the point

Most sanctioned events and closed courses start from a simple position: no aerial filming unless the organizer has approved it in advance, in writing, for a named crew on a named day. That is not red tape to work around. It is how a promoter protects a crowd, a field of competitors, and a program that has to run on time. If an operator treats that rule as a suggestion, you learn everything you need to know about how they will treat every other rule on your day.

So the first question is never what camera flies or what the edit looks like. The first question is who grants permission, how far ahead they need to see a plan, and what they need in that plan before they will say yes. When a promoter in this region builds a season calendar, they are looking at weekends like the weekly programs at I-29 Speedway and Dragway in Pacific Junction, the dirt oval nights at Eagle Raceway outside Lincoln, and the road course weekends at Motorsport Park Hastings. Each of those places runs its own program its own way. There is no universal pass, and anyone who promises you one is guessing with your event.

StoryArc works sanctioned events and closed courses with organizer permission only. No permission means no flight, full stop. That sentence will cost us work some weekends, and we put it in writing anyway, because a promoter who reads it knows exactly what they are hiring: a crew that would rather lose a day than put their event at risk.

What an approval request actually contains

A serious request is short, specific, and sent early enough for the organizer to say no and for you to accept that answer. It names the event, the date, and the parts of the day where flying is requested. It shows where the crew will launch and land, well clear of the racing surface, the pits as a work area, and every place spectators gather. It shows the flight lines the crew proposes, and just as clearly, the lines it will not cross.

It also covers the practical questions a race director will ask before anything creative comes up:

  • Who is in charge on the ground. One named person the organizer and race control can reach, stop, and move at any moment.
  • When flying stops. During incidents, during recovery, during any pause the officials call, and whenever the wind or the crowd picture changes.
  • Where the aircraft never goes. Over spectators, over an active hot pit, and over competitors in a way that could distract or endanger them.
  • What the event gets back. The footage the organizer can use for promotion, agreed before the day, not argued about after it.
  • How the day is checked before anyone arrives. The location and airspace check is free, and for a track day that check includes the venue permission picture, not just a map.

Notice what is not in that list: promises about how dramatic the footage will be. Drama is the last thing an organizer should be sold before the safety picture is settled. The creative conversation belongs after approval, when everyone knows what can actually be flown. Our action and motorsports coverage is built in that order on purpose: permission first, plan second, flying third.

What an approved crew does differently on the day

Approval changes the whole shape of the work. An approved crew arrives with a briefed plan instead of a hope. It checks in with the organizer and with race control before the first flight, confirms the launch area and the no fly lines for that day, and agrees the signal that grounds the aircraft immediately. If the program changes, and race programs always change, the plan changes with it and the crew waits rather than improvises over a live event.

The flying itself looks calmer than people expect. Passes are planned around the racing, not across it. The crew flies in the gaps the schedule gives it, lands when officials need the air clear, and treats a missed shot as a small price next to a clean day. Ground coverage of the pits, the grid, and the crowd often carries as much of the finished film as the chase work, and it carries none of the risk, which is why a good plan leans on it heavily.

Weather sits over all of this in Nebraska and Iowa. Spring and fall weekends can turn windy fast enough to ground small aircraft, and summer heat changes how a long day should be paced. An approved crew tells the organizer early when the wind picture is going the wrong way, and weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer when a day has to move. A crew that will only admit a problem once it is on site is telling you its plan was never real.

There is also an honest limit worth stating plainly. Some events should not have aerial coverage at all: a layout with no safe launch area clear of people, a program too tight to brief properly, or an organizer who cannot spare anyone to coordinate on the day. In those cases the right answer is ground coverage only, or no coverage, and we will say so before you spend anything. That is a sale we would rather lose than a day we would rather forget.

How to start, and when

Start with the organizer, not with us. If you are the organizer, the conversation is simple: send the event, the date, and the shape of the day, and ask what your own approval process needs. If you are a team or a sponsor who wants coverage at an event you do not run, your first job is to get the organizer to agree in principle, because no crew can grant itself permission at someone else's track.

Start earlier than feels necessary. Approval lead time is the one part of this work you cannot buy back later, and a request that lands the week of the event puts the organizer in a position where no is the only responsible answer. A request that lands a month ahead, with a clear plan and clear limits, gives a promoter something they can actually approve.

Price comes after permission, because the approved shape of the day is what the price describes. Once you know what can be flown, what race day aerial coverage costs becomes a plain conversation about days, deliverables, and delivery dates. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, including what the event itself can use, so the approval and the paperwork tell the same story.
