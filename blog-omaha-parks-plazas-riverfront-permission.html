Parks, Plazas, and the RiverFront: Getting Permission for Omaha's Public Spaces

A public park feels like it belongs to everyone, so it is easy to assume a drone can simply show up and fly. In Omaha, public never means unmanaged. Here is who grants permission for the city's favorite filming spaces, and how to get a real answer before you plan around one.

September 2026

Public space, real gatekeepers

Picture the shoot you have in mind. It might be a family festival on a park lawn, a wedding party arriving at a garden, a morning film over the RiverFront with the skyline behind it. Every one of those places has a name, a calendar, and someone whose job is to protect it. Memorial Park fills with picnics and pickup games. Elmwood Park hosts runners, golfers, and neighborhood events. The RiverFront parks downtown, including the Gene Leahy Mall and Heartland of America Park, run programmed events through much of the year.

That management is good news for a buyer, not bad news. It means there is a real person or office who can give you a real answer, with conditions you can plan around. The mistake is skipping the ask and treating an open gate as permission. An open gate is an invitation to visit. A flight is work happening in that space, and work needs a yes.

Who says yes, and what they usually ask

The permission route depends on the kind of space, and sorting out which route applies is the first task on any public space shoot.

  • City parks. The city parks system manages the lawns, shelters, and trails, and permission for filming runs through the offices that manage park use and events. Expect questions about date, hours, crowd size, and where you intend to take off and land.
  • The RiverFront. The downtown riverfront parks are managed as event spaces in their own right, with their own calendars and conditions. A shoot there is planned with the team that runs the space, especially when another event shares your date.
  • Plazas and privately held open spaces. Many plazas that feel public are owned by a building, a district, or a business. The owner or property manager grants permission, and their answer may involve their tenants, their security team, and their own rules about hours.
  • Events already holding the space. If a festival, market, or wedding has reserved the ground, the organizer's plans come first. A flight joins that plan only with the organizer's agreement and a path that stays clear of their guests.

Airspace still sits above all of this. Ground permission and airspace authorization are two separate yeses, and a downtown park can need both.

You are never left to guess whether the ask happened. We tell you who granted permission and what conditions came with it.

Asking early matters more than most buyers expect. Park offices and venue teams plan around weddings, reunions, races, markets, and maintenance, and a request that arrives weeks ahead lands on a desk that can still shape the day around it. A request that arrives days ahead may meet a calendar that is already full, even when everyone involved would like to help. Early asking also leaves time to choose a better hour, arrange a quieter corner of the grounds, and warn regular users of the space that filming will happen.

Conditions are normal and usually workable.

A manager may limit flights to early morning before the park wakes up, keep takeoff and landing inside a marked corner away from paths, or ask that flights pause while a ceremony or a game is underway. A good flight plan treats those conditions as choreography, not obstacles. Filming at first light in an empty park often produces the best footage anyway: long shadows, calm air, and the space exactly as its designers hoped it would look.

When the answer is no, or not that day

The honest part: public permission is sometimes refused, and we accept that answer even when it costs us the shoot. A park may be closed for reseeding, a plaza may be under construction, or your date may collide with a city event that cannot share the sky. During College World Series weeks, downtown spaces carry crowds and schedules that change what any responsible operator should attempt.

A no for one space is rarely a no for the film. Omaha is generous with settings: another park in Papillion or Bellevue, a private venue with a simpler permission route, or the same park on a quieter morning can carry the story just as well. What we will not do is fly first and apologize later. Your event, your organization's name, and your relationship with the people who manage these shared spaces all outlast a single shot, and they are worth more than it.
