Half Day or Full Day? How Drone Coverage Is Actually Sold

Buyers naturally think in hours. An hour of filming sounds like something you should be able to buy, the way you buy an hour of anything else. Aerial coverage is sold in blocks instead, and once you see why the block exists, and what it quietly includes, choosing between a half day and a full day becomes a straightforward decision.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Why coverage is sold in blocks, not hours

A shoot consumes more than the minutes the drone is in the air. There is the location and airspace check before the day, travel and setup, the walking of routes and positions, the weather watch, battery and gear management through the day, and the file handling afterward. Just as important, a booked block removes that time from every other buyer. An operator holding your Saturday in June, in the middle of Omaha event season, cannot sell those hours twice. The block is the honest unit because the day is the honest cost.

Published regional practice reflects this. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day, while national production support commonly runs $1,500 to $2,000 and up per day. Event coverage with us is priced by the half day or full day, and action and motorsports coverage by the event day. Our cost guide sets those figures in context by project type, so you can see which family your need belongs to.

What a half day block usually fits

  • A single location with a tight purpose. A venue flythrough package, a business film sequence, a focused property story.
  • An event with one clear peak. A ceremony, a grand opening moment, a race feature, where the essential coverage sits inside a few connected hours.
  • A production sequence. A set of establishing and aerial shots a visiting crew needs within one part of their day.

What a full day block fits is the opposite shape: a story with an arc. Festivals that build from gates to headline set. Event days during College World Series weeks, when the energy worth capturing runs from morning crowds to evening lights along the RiverFront. Race days on sanctioned closed courses, where practice, heats, and finals each carry part of the story a sponsor cut will later need. Productions moving between locations across the metro, from the Old Market to a site in Elkhorn, where travel and reset time are real parts of the work.

The questions that reveal the right block

Ask when the block starts. Some operators start the clock at arrival on site, others at first flight, and the difference matters on a day with a slow build. Ask what happens when the day runs long, and get the extra block price in the written quote rather than discovering it at hour five with the headliner still to come. Ask whether the block includes the edit or covers coverage only. Event blocks commonly pair the day with a recap film and sponsor ready cuts, while production blocks commonly deliver files to the production editor, with production files from us within two business days. The block buys time. The deliverables are a separate line, and both belong in writing with the delivery date and usage rights.

Also ask the wind question. Nebraska weather does not respect blocks. If high wind grounds the morning, does the block slide, pause, or burn? Our standing policy is plain: weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer. Whatever operator you choose, get their version of that sentence in the quote.

Choosing honestly, including choosing less

The sentence that costs us: many buyers who ask for a full day need a half day shot well, and we will argue them down to it when the shape of their event says so. A two hour ceremony with a one hour reception peak does not become better coverage because someone bought eight hours of it. It becomes a longer bill and a tired edit.

Decide by the arc, not the anxiety. List the moments the film cannot miss, note the gaps between them, and choose the block that covers the moments with a small margin. Then put the block, its start rule, its overrun price, and its deliverables in writing through the quote page, and the day can run long on energy without running long on surprises.

Geography quietly shapes this choice in the metro. A full day spent at one Bellevue venue uses the block very differently from a full day split between a morning in the Old Market and an afternoon in Elkhorn, where travel, parking, and a second setup consume the hours between the moments you actually want filmed. When you describe your day, describe its map as well as its schedule. An operator who sees both can tell you honestly whether one block covers the ground or whether the plan itself needs simplifying, and that conversation, held before booking, is far cheaper than watching a block dissolve into driving.

If you are comparing two operators on blocks, compare the block definitions before the block prices. A cheaper half day that starts at first flight and ends four hours later is not the same product as a half day that includes setup and a sensible margin. Definitions first, totals second, exactly as with every other line in a written quote.
