The One Take Is a Promise: Choreography, Rehearsal, and the Continuous Shot

Every cut in a film asks the viewer to trust the editor. A continuous shot asks for a different kind of trust, the trust that nothing was hidden. That is why the one take matters, and why it falls apart the moment it becomes a stunt.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

What the promise actually is

When I fly a one take tour of a space, I am making a claim that editing cannot make. I am saying this place really connects the way you are seeing it connect. The entrance really leads to that room. The room really opens onto that view. Nothing was rearranged in the timeline to flatter the layout, because there was no timeline trick available. For a couple judging a wedding venue outside Elkhorn, or a family weighing a campus they have never visited, that honesty is not a style choice. It is the whole value of the film.

I explain the format itself in my plain English guide to what an FPV flythrough is, but the philosophy deserves its own argument. A cut is a small act of persuasion. A continuous shot is a small act of testimony. Both have their place in filmmaking, and I use cuts gladly in most of my work. But the flythrough earns its premium, in trust rather than money, by surrendering the editor's favorite tool. What remains has to be true in the air, in one unbroken pass, or it is nothing.

Plenty of filmmakers will disagree, and some already have, in conversations I value. They argue that viewers cannot tell the difference and that a well edited sequence delivers the same feeling with less risk. They are right about the feeling and wrong about the contract. Feelings can be manufactured. The promise cannot. The moment a venue learns its tour was assembled from fragments, the viewer's trust in every future film from that venue drops a little. The one take protects something larger than itself.

Choreography is the unglamorous truth

People imagine a one take flight as a burst of talent. It is mostly choreography and rehearsal, and the rehearsal is where the film is actually made. I walk the route until I know where the doors swing, where the light falls off, where a reflection in glass will betray the aircraft, and where the shot needs to slow down because the space is asking a question the viewer needs a second to answer.

Then I fly it in pieces, section by section, the way dancers learn a routine. The entrance until it is boring. The turn until it no longer drifts. The final reveal until the timing lands without a correction. Only then do the pieces join. On the day, what looks like spontaneity is a rehearsed sequence performed at the speed of calm. The audience should feel a host walking them through a beloved place, never a pilot surviving a route.

People in the space are part of the choreography too. A staff member who knows when to step aside, a door held by someone who understands the timing, a moment where the flight passes a working kitchen or a busy bar and the life of the place enters the frame on cue. None of that is improvised. All of it is agreed in advance, in plain language, during the walk. The one take is a promise the whole building helps keep.

Rehearsal also reveals what the route is quietly hiding. Every space has one section that flatters itself in a walk and betrays itself in the air: a dark corridor between two bright rooms where the camera hunts for exposure, a glass wall that turns the aircraft into an unwanted actor, a stairwell where the natural line pulls wide exactly where the building narrows. Finding those sections early is a mercy for everyone. On shoot day they are solved problems. Without rehearsal they become the reason a promised one take quietly turns into three takes and an apology, and the film loses the very trust it was built to earn. I would rather spend an unglamorous hour discovering a building's dishonest corners in private than ask a client to watch me discover them in public. That hour is not overhead. It is the fee the promise charges, and paying it gladly is what separates a rehearsed flight from a hopeful one.

When the promise should be broken

Now the part that costs me. I do not believe every space should be filmed in one take, and I will recommend against it when the building argues otherwise. Some spaces are genuinely disjointed, and a forced continuous flight turns into a long, seasick corridor crawl that flatters nothing. Some stories need contrast, a before and an after, an exterior at dawn and an interior at dusk, that no single pass can honestly contain. Insisting on one take there does not serve the truth. It serves the pilot's portfolio.

There is a subtler failure too. A one take that calls attention to its own difficulty has already broken the spirit of the promise. If the viewer spends the film marveling at the flying, the space has lost. I have scrapped flights I was personally proud of for exactly that reason. The pass was flawless and the film was wrong, because the aircraft had become the subject. The delete key is part of the choreography. Any honest account of this format has to include the takes that never ship.

When the format fits, though, nothing else does its work. The range of spaces where a continuous flight serves a venue is set out on our services page, and the common thread is connection. Spaces whose value lies in how their parts flow together are the spaces the promise was made for. Book the one take for those. Let the other buildings be filmed in honest pieces, and let no one, pilot or venue, pretend the two approaches are interchangeable. The promise only means something if you are willing to decline it.
