Building an Aerial Library for an Agency: Stock Days and Repeat Locations

Agencies rarely lack footage. They lack the right footage, cleared for the right use, findable in the week a pitch or campaign needs it. A planned aerial library fixes that, but only when it is built from client needs rather than a desire to collect pretty views.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Every agency has a drive full of almost useful aerial clips. The light is wrong, the building has since changed, the usage was never cleared for paid media, or no one can find the version without a logo in frame. A library built on purpose looks different. It starts with the clients and categories you serve, names the locations and seasons you return to, and captures each place in the versions future work will actually need. Stock days then become production days with a longer memory.

Start with demand, not locations

List the aerial requests your team made in the last year, including the ones you solved with generic stock and wished you had not. Group them by job. Openers for pitches. Place setting for property, venue, and destination work. Industrial scale for manufacturing and logistics clients. Seasonal beauty for campaigns that return each year. That demand list decides the shoot list. A library built from it will be used. A library built from a map of attractive places will be admired and ignored.

Omaha and the metro give an agency a compact set of repeat locations with real range. Downtown and the RiverFront for city identity. The Old Market for hospitality and retail texture. Rail yards and industrial areas for working scale. Suburban growth in Gretna, Papillion, Bellevue, and Elkhorn for development and campus stories. Open land and river edges for space and calm. The point is not to film everything. It is to film the places your clients return to, in the light and season their audiences recognise.

How a stock day is planned

A stock day is a production day with a library brief. The shot list names each location, the move, the versions needed, and the future use in mind. Capture the clean wide, the closer detail pass, and a version with negative space for titles where that will help design. Note any business signs, people, or vehicles that limit use. Build the route around light, so east facing subjects are not all captured in flat midday sun, and leave room for the one unplanned view that only appears when you are in the air.

The day still follows normal production discipline. Permissions and airspace are checked per location, with Eppley shaping plans near downtown and the river. A location that was clear last spring is not assumed clear this autumn. The planning model on our page for filmmakers applies in full, and our guide to briefing an aerial unit will help an agency producer write a library brief that editors can actually use.

  • Versions per location. Wide, closer pass, and title friendly space where useful.
  • Season record. Date, light, and conditions logged so future users know the context.
  • Use notes. Signs, people, and limits flagged at capture, not discovered in a pitch.
  • Searchable names. Location, subject, and version in every file name from delivery.

Rights and organisation decide the value

A library without clear rights is a liability in a folder. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and library work needs that clause written with extra care. State whether the footage is for one client, for agency pitch use, or for a defined group of uses, and what happens when a clip moves from a pitch into paid work. That clarity protects the agency when a client falls in love with a shot from a deck and asks to run it nationally.

Organisation is the second half of value. Production files are delivered within two business days, organised and labelled, with a log that connects files to locations and standout takes. Store the log with the media, keep original files untouched, and review the library on a set rhythm. Replies during planning come within one business day, so an agency can test a stock day idea against a live campaign calendar before committing crew time.

The honest warning about libraries

Libraries decay, and anyone selling you one should say so. Buildings change, brands re sign storefronts, a new development alters a familiar view, and a clip that felt current can date through vehicles, clothing, or a skyline detail. Generic aerial stock also has a ceiling. It can set a place, but it cannot show your client place, your client team, or the specific proof a campaign needs. For hero work, commissioned filming will beat library footage almost every time.

That does not make a library a poor buy. It makes it a supporting asset with a clear role. Use it for pitches, proposals, internal films, background texture, and fast turnaround work where a specific shoot cannot happen in time. Refresh the most used locations, retire the clips that no longer tell the truth, and commission new filming when the footage must carry the idea. An agency that treats its aerial library that way stops renting the same establishing shot in different forms. It owns a memory of the places it sells, ready before the brief arrives.
