The Sponsor Inventory You Already Own: Selling Cuts From Flights You Have Paid For

You already pay to cover your event, and your sponsors already appear in that footage on banners, cars, bikes, and podiums, but none of it reaches them in a form they can use. You are sitting on inventory you have already paid to create, and most promoters never sell it because nobody packaged it.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

One flight can honestly serve three buyers

Start with the economics that make this work. A covered race day produces a main recap for the event, material for teams and racers, and, inside the same footage, clean views of every sponsor who showed up on a banner, a hauler, a machine, or a podium backdrop. The flying does not need to happen three times. The editing and the rights need to be planned three ways, and that planning is cheap compared to the day itself.

That is the inventory: short sponsor cuts, cut from flights you already commissioned, that show a backer's name inside a living event, with a crowd, a field, and a result around it. A sponsor can post it, send it to their own customers, and show it internally as proof their money was visible and working. For many local and regional sponsors, that cut is worth more than the logo placement itself, because the placement ends when the weekend ends and the film keeps working all year.

The setting matters to the value. This inventory only exists at sanctioned events and closed courses covered with organizer permission only, because a sponsor will only use footage they are comfortable being associated with. Approved coverage, briefed with the organizer and run inside the event's rules, produces material a business can publish without a second thought. The discipline behind that is set out in how drone coverage gets permission at a race track, and it is part of what you are selling a sponsor even when they never ask about it directly.

What the inventory actually looks like

  • The banner and venue cut. A short film of the facility on a live day with sponsor signage in its natural place, proving presence rather than claiming it.
  • The machine cut. For a sponsor attached to a car, bike, or team, a focused cut of that machine across the day, with their marks readable and the racing real.
  • The podium and people cut. Winners, crowds, and celebration with branding in frame, the material sponsors most often choose for their own channels.
  • The season cut. The same sponsor shown across multiple rounds, which turns a single weekend claim into a season story and supports renewal conversations better than any report.
  • The hospitality cut. Suites, displays, and activation areas shown busy and working, for sponsors whose return is measured in guests and leads rather than in exposure alone.

Our action and motorsports coverage can scope these cuts as part of an event day or a season, but the packaging decision belongs to the promoter. You know what each sponsor paid, what they value, and what would renew them. The coverage crew's job is to film with those cuts in mind, which means knowing the sponsor list before the day, not discovering it in the edit.

The part that must be settled in writing first

Here is the limit that stops many promoters, and it is better learned here than in a dispute. You can only sell or give a sponsor a cut if the usage rights for that use were agreed when the coverage was commissioned. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and sponsor distribution should be named in that document from the start: which sponsors, which cuts, which channels, and for how long.

If your current coverage was commissioned for the event's own channels only, then the honest position is that the sponsor inventory is not yours to sell yet, however good the footage looks. Renegotiating rights after the fact is possible but awkward, and it costs more than agreeing them up front ever does. This is the sentence that costs us the easy upsell: do not promise a sponsor a cut until the rights to give it to them exist in writing. Promoters who follow that rule keep both their sponsors and their crews. Promoters who skip it eventually lose one of the two.

How to price and present it without overselling

Present the inventory as part of the sponsorship conversation, not as a surprise afterward. A sponsor package that includes a film of their presence, delivered on a stated date, is easier to renew than a package described only in signage and announcements, because the sponsor leaves with something their whole business can see. Price it as the value it is: real editing, real rights, and material with a year of use in it. Giving sponsor cuts away free to be nice usually teaches sponsors the film is worth nothing, and the next negotiation starts from there.

Also keep the promise inside what one day of footage can honestly support. A sponsor whose signage was small and whose team had a quiet weekend will have a thinner cut, and overselling them a starring role produces a disappointed renewal conversation. Show samples of the shape, name the deliverables plainly, and let the footage be what the day actually gave you. In a regional paddock, where the same sponsors and promoters meet at tracks from Pacific Junction to Hastings to Lincoln across a whole season, a reputation for straight dealing on small things like this is worth more than any single package you will sell this year.
