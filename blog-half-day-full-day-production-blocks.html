Half Day or Full Day Blocks: How Production Aerial Is Scheduled

The block you book decides more than price. It decides whether the best light, the talent window, and the hardest move can share one plan, or whether the day becomes a negotiation against the clock. Booking the right block is schedule protection, not upselling.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Production aerial is sold in blocks because a shoot day is not a pile of minutes. It is call, setup, safety checks, rehearsal where needed, flights in a planned order, resets, and file care at wrap. A half day and a full day are different containers for that work. The right choice depends on locations, light, company moves, and how tightly the aerial must sync with talent and ground coverage. Get it right and the aerial feels calm. Get it wrong and every department feels the squeeze.

What a half day block covers well

A half day suits one location, a contained shot list, and a clear light window. Think of a single venue sequence, a set of establishing shots around one site, or an interior to exterior move that can be rehearsed and captured without moving the unit across town. The creative call, shot design, location and airspace check, and delivery for post are part of the work around the block, and production files are delivered within two business days.

Published context helps planning. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day. National production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 per day, and higher for larger productions. Those figures are a planning frame, not a promise that every half day costs exactly half of every creative problem. A short block with a difficult move can demand more preparation than a long block of simple coverage.

  • Strong half day fit. One location, one light window, up to a focused set of moves.
  • Needs discipline. Boards approved, marks set, and talent ready when the unit is.
  • Weak fit. Two suburbs, a sunrise and a sunset idea, and a client still choosing shots.

What a full day block protects

A full day protects choice. It allows a morning and a late day light window, a company move between locations such as downtown Omaha and a site in Bellevue or Papillion, and time to respond when the ground schedule shifts. It also allows the aerial unit to wait for the moment the production actually needs, instead of forcing flights into the only hour available. For commercials where the opening aerial carries client approval, that waiting time is not waste. It is insurance.

Full day scheduling also helps when the aerial must interlock with other departments. Art may need to dress between passes. Talent may run a route on cue. A location in the Old Market may have delivery traffic early and quiet streets later. A longer block lets the producer place the aerial where it helps the whole call sheet, rather than where a short booking happens to end. Splitting time, light, and weather

Producers sometimes ask whether a full day can be split into a sunrise call and a sunset return. Sometimes it can, and sometimes the travel, setup, and permission conditions make two half days the cleaner purchase. The honest answer depends on location and season. Nebraska summer gives long days and strong midday glare that few aerial shots love. Winter gives short windows and wind that can close a plan early. Name the light you need in the consult, and the block can be shaped around it in writing.

Weather sits across every block. We watch the forecast with you starting days out. If flying conditions fall apart, the aerial block is rescheduled rather than forced, and weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer. A full day does not buy immunity from wind. What it buys is more room to move the aerial order inside the day when a calm window appears.

The false economy to avoid

Here is the cost we will state even when a larger booking would suit us. If your shot list is truly two simple exteriors at one location, book the half day. A full day adds no value when the work is done well by midday. The reverse warning matters more often. Booking a half day for a full day problem does not save money when it forces a second call, a new permission process, or an edit built from rushed coverage.

Read the schedule for warning signs. The call sheet has two locations. The client wants to review playback and add shots. The talent window is uncertain. The location sits near Eppley Airfield and needs early flagging of any design change. Any two of those together point to a full day. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and the block length will be justified against your call sheet in plain words. Replies come within one business day, so you can test the fit before the schedule hardens.
