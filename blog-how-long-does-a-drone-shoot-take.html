How Long Does a Drone Shoot Actually Take? Honest Timings by Project Type

Buyers plan around their own calendars, so how long will this take is really three questions. How long are you here? How much of my day does it consume? And when do I actually have the finished film? Here are honest answers for each project type, including the parts that happen before and after the flying.

December 2025

The shoot itself, by project type

  • Single location flythrough. One to two hours on site for most venues and businesses. That includes a walk of the route, small adjustments, and several flown passes so the edit has choices. The filming itself is the short part. The planning that makes it short happened earlier.
  • Brand or business film with aerial. Usually a half day or full day, depending on how many setups, people, and locations the story needs. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day, and those blocks exist because brand work expands to fill the story you agree, which is why the scope belongs in writing.
  • Event coverage. Booked by the half day or full day, matched to the arc of the event rather than the clock alone. A festival evening or a race day has a shape, gates, peak, and close, and coverage is planned around capturing that shape whole.
  • Aerial unit for a production. Booked in day blocks inside the production schedule, with arrival, setup, and standby built around the call sheet rather than around our convenience.

The pattern underneath the numbers is simple. Simple subjects film fast once planned. Anything involving people, schedules, or a date that cannot move takes the time it takes, and a quote that promises it dramatically faster is usually planning to skip the planning. The time before the shoot that you barely see

Before anyone flies, the location and airspace check runs, and it is free. For an open site near Gretna that can be quick. For a downtown Omaha address near Eppley Airfield or a RiverFront venue, permissions and coordination take longer, sometimes days of lead time even though the work itself is a small part of a day. Then comes the flight path or shot list, the venue timing conversation, and the weather watch as the day approaches. Nebraska wind is checked more than once, because a forecast at booking and a forecast on the morning can tell two different stories.

Your time in this phase is measured in minutes. An address, a short description of the outcome, access on the day, and a contact name. Buyers sometimes apologize for not having more prepared. More is rarely needed. Preparation is the product you are buying.

The time after the shoot, until the film is yours

Editing is where most of the calendar actually goes, and it is the phase impatient quotes go quiet about. Our standard is finished films within five business days, and production files within two business days for productions whose editors are waiting. Your written quote carries the actual delivery date, which is the date to build a launch, a listing, or a sponsor delivery around.

Rush delivery can compress that window when an event film needs to ride the week of the event itself, and it costs more, because editing hours stack into evenings to make it happen. Ask for it before booking if you need it. Named early, it is a priced option. Discovered late, it is a stressful negotiation.

Build one more allowance into your own planning. Weather can move a shoot day, and during the busiest stretches, College World Series weeks in Omaha or the height of fall venue booking, replacement windows fill faster than in a quiet month. Booking with a little slack is not pessimism. It is how a shoot stays a calm two hours instead of a scramble.

The honesty line: we will not promise a timing we cannot hold to win a booking. If your date is too tight for the film you want, we will say so at the quote stage, and sometimes the honest answer is a smaller scope that fits the date, or no shoot at all.

Give us your date and your address through the quote page, and we will tell you which timing shape your project falls into, in writing, before you plan around a guess.

It also helps to separate the clock you feel from the clock that matters. The clock you feel is the visit itself, the hour or two of a flythrough or the day of an event, and it passes quickly. The clock that matters is the one between your first message and a published film working on your homepage, and that span is mostly planning and editing, measured in days. Plan your launch, your listing, or your sponsor delivery against the second clock, confirm it in the written quote, and the first clock can stay what it should be: a brief, well run interruption in an otherwise ordinary working day at your Omaha business.

When in doubt, ask the operator to state the total span in plain words on the quote itself: arrival time, expected finish on site, and the calendar date the finished film will be in your hands. Three short facts, written down, end nearly every timing argument before it can begin.
