White Label Aerial: Your Client Stays Yours

Bringing in an outside aerial unit raises one quiet fear. Will the client start seeing the subcontractor as the creative partner, and your company as the middleman? White label work exists to remove that fear, but only when the rules are agreed in writing before the shoot.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

You built the client relationship over months or years. You pitched, you revised, you carried the risk on earlier jobs. An aerial unit that arrives for one day should strengthen that relationship, never borrow from it. That is the whole point of white label aerial. The unit works as part of your production, under your brand, with communication routed through you, and hands every file to you for delivery. Done properly, the client experiences a stronger production company. Done loosely, it creates confusion about who owns the work and who the client should call next time.

What white label means on the day

It starts with small disciplines that signal respect. The unit wears plain clothing or your production clothing if you provide it. No separate business cards change hands. No portfolio conversation happens with the client unless you invite it. Questions from the client about schedule, scope, or price are routed back to your producer with a simple line. That decision sits with the production, and I will connect you now.

On set, the unit still does its full job. It attends the briefing, confirms marks and safety lanes, flies the agreed lines, and logs takes for your editor. If a client asks for an extra shot that changes scope, the unit notes the request and checks with your producer before flying it. That pause protects your budget conversation. It also protects the client, because an informal yes on set can become an awkward invoice later if no one named the change.

The service model behind this is set out on our page for filmmakers, where white label work is named plainly as welcome. It pairs with our guide to briefing an aerial unit, because a white label day still needs the same shot intent and delivery agreement as any other production day.

Files, credit, and the handover

All files go to you, in your naming pattern where you provide one, organised and labelled, with the flight log included. Production files are delivered within two business days as standard. You decide what the client sees, when they see it, and whether any credit appears. If you edit the sequence in house, the client receives your finished work. If you ask us to cut the aerial sequence, it is delivered to you first for approval, never sent direct to the client unless you instruct that in writing.

  • Communication. One channel, through your producer, unless you name a wider group.
  • Branding. No separate signage, cards, or social posts about your shoot.
  • Files. Delivered to you only, with usage terms as listed in the written quote.
  • Future contact. Your client is not added to any list and is not approached for separate work.

Usage belongs in the same written agreement. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. In white label work that clarity matters even more, because the end client may later want the aerial in paid media, broadcast, or a new campaign. The quote should state whose usage is covered and what happens when the use grows.

Boundaries that keep white label honest

Here is the limit we state up front, even though it would be easier to leave it vague. White label governs branding and client contact. It does not mean hiding who operated the aircraft from a venue, a property owner, or an authority that needs to know for permission and safety. If a location near Eppley Airfield or a RiverFront venue requires the operator to be identified during the permission process, that identification happens through the proper channel. Your client relationship stays protected. The safety record stays accurate. Both can be true at once.

There is a second honesty point. If you need the aerial unit to remain invisible even in the credits your own crew receives, say so before booking, because some productions list all department contributors as a matter of policy. Agreeing credit in advance avoids a difficult edit at delivery. We would rather have that short conversation early than surprise you in the titles.

When white label is the wrong choice

Sometimes the stronger move is open collaboration. If your client hired you partly for the specialists you bring, naming the aerial unit as a creative partner can raise confidence rather than dilute it. That is your call to make, not ours. What we will not do is blur the arrangement halfway, where the client is unsure who is responsible for the aerial if a shot fails or a file is late.

Omaha productions often run lean, with a director, a small ground crew, and a client watching playback in suburbs such as Elkhorn or Bellevue. In that setting, clarity is kindness. Name the structure, write it into the quote, and brief the whole set on it at call time. Your client stays yours because the work was designed that way from the first conversation, not because anyone hoped it would turn out fine.
