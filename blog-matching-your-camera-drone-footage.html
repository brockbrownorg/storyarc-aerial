Matching Your Camera: Settings, Profiles, and File Handover That Editors Trust

Editors can smell a mismatch in one cut. The aerial arrives brighter, sharper, and warmer than the ground work, and the grade that flatters one breaks the other. Matching is fixable, but only when it is planned before the shoot and described honestly.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Matching does not mean making an aerial camera into your main camera. It means giving your editor files that sit comfortably beside the ground footage, with the same motion feel, a compatible colour starting point, and enough information in the file to grade without fighting it. When productions skip this conversation, post inherits the problem. When they have it early, the aerial often drops into the timeline with little more than the normal grade.

Start by sharing what your editor actually needs, not what sounds technical. Frame rate, resolution, shutter approach, white balance practice, and the profile you shoot on the ground are the core facts. Add how the film will finish, such as a soft natural grade, a high contrast commercial look, or a broadcast delivery with strict levels. That context decides how flat the aerial should be and how much sharpening or saturation to leave out in camera.

The settings that matter most

Frame rate and shutter shape motion, and motion mismatch is the hardest flaw to hide. If the ground unit shoots at a given frame rate for normal speed work, the aerial should follow that plan for shots that will cut directly together. Flight speed also changes the feel, so brief any shot that must feel calm, urgent, or weightless. A slow rise over the Missouri River at first light reads differently from a fast push across a car park in Gretna, even at identical camera settings.

White balance deserves a fixed decision rather than a floating one. Agree whether the aerial holds a set balance for a scene or shifts with changing light between interior and exterior passes. Exposure discipline matters as much. Bright Nebraska summer skies can tempt a setting that protects the sky and buries the ground in shadow. Tell the unit what subject must hold detail. Usually it is the building entrance, the talent, or the product, not the clouds.

  • Motion. Frame rate, shutter approach, and intended flight speed per sequence.
  • Colour start. Flat or natural profile, saturation and contrast held back for the grade.
  • Balance and exposure. Fixed choices per scene, with the priority subject named.
  • Filters and light. Any neutral density plan agreed for bright days and reflective surfaces.

Test frames and the creative call

For work where the match carries the scene, shoot test frames against ground footage on the day or in a short scout where the schedule allows. Compare skin, foliage, brick, and sky if those elements appear in the film. The Old Market gives an editor every test at once, with red brick, glass, shadowed streets, and open sky in one move. A two minute review at the monitor can save a long corrective session later.

This step belongs in the same planning described on our page for filmmakers and in our guide to briefing an aerial unit. Camera settings and colour approach are agreed in the creative call, not guessed at the location. If your production has a colourist, invite that voice into the call for five minutes. Editors and colourists ask sharper questions than anyone else, and their answers belong in the brief.

What matching cannot do

Honesty matters here, because overselling a match helps no one. An aerial camera on a small FPV aircraft or a compact aerial camera will not reproduce the depth, lens character, or low light behaviour of a larger ground camera. It cannot invent highlight detail that was clipped in bright sky, and it cannot fully remove the different feel of a very wide field of view when the ground work uses longer lenses. Stabilisation in post can smooth a move, but heavy correction can also bend straight lines and soften the frame.

What matching can do is narrow the gap until the cut feels intentional. It can align motion, protect the important tones, keep colour restrained enough for a shared grade, and avoid in camera choices that fight post. If a hero shot must match perfectly because talent performance and aerial movement share one continuous beat, say so early. The honest answer may be to redesign the shot, change the time of day, or let the ground unit carry that beat while the aerial covers the wider context.

A handover editors can trust

Files should arrive organised, labelled, and accompanied by the notes an editor needs without a phone call. State the profile and colour space in plain words in the delivery note, list any shots captured with different settings and why, and flag takes where light changed mid move. Production files are delivered within two business days as standard. Every written quote lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, so the handover is measured against a promise made before the shoot.

Keep the original files untouched alongside any corrected selects if selects are part of the order. An editor who can return to the clean original trusts the delivery more, not less. Matching, in the end, is a trust practice. Share the ground truth early, test where the risk is highest, and write down what was actually captured. Your grade will still need craft. It will not need rescue.
