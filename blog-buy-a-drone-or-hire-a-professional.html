Buy a Drone or Hire a Professional? The Honest Math for a Business

Sooner or later every business owner does the same sum. A capable drone costs less than a single professional shoot, so why not buy one and film everything yourself? Sometimes that sum is right, and we will show you when. But the purchase price is only the first line of the real cost, and the rest of the ledger decides the answer.

December 2025

The case for buying, stated fairly

Buying makes genuine sense for some businesses. If you need simple aerial views often, a construction firm documenting progress monthly, a large property recording seasonal change, a team that shoots rough internal updates no client will ever judge, then owning a drone spreads its cost across dozens of flights and the footage standard you need is forgiving. A handy staff member may enjoy the work, and enjoyable work gets done. For internal records, progress documentation, and quick social clips where polish is optional, ownership can be the smart, thrifty choice.

We mean that without a wink. If your need is frequent, simple, and internal, buy the drone. Hiring us for that work would charge you film prices for record keeping, and telling you so costs us those bookings. The companion question in whether drone video is worth it for a business like yours makes the same point from the other side: the value question comes before the vendor question, always.

The costs the purchase price hides

  • Learning time. Flying safely is learned in hours. Flying cinematically, smooth lines, calm reveals, controlled indoor work, is learned in seasons. The gap shows on screen immediately, and your customers will judge the footage, not the effort.
  • Editing time. The shoot is the short part. Selecting, coloring, cutting, music, and versions for each platform take hours per film, at the hourly value of whoever on your team gives them up.
  • Permissions and checks. Every new location raises airspace and venue permission questions. Near Eppley Airfield or downtown Omaha, those questions take real work to answer properly, and the responsibility sits with whoever flies.
  • Gear beyond the drone. Extra batteries, spare parts after the inevitable knock, software, storage, and eventually the newer model, because this category of equipment ages quickly.
  • The crash and the quiet failure. A drone that hits a tree line in Nebraska wind is a budget event. A drone that simply stops being flown, because the enthusiastic staff member got busy, is the more common one. Most business drones end as shelf equipment, and a shelf drone has the worst cost per flight of all.

The math that actually decides it

Run the comparison on cost per finished, published film, not cost per flight. Count the purchase, the learning hours at a real wage, the editing hours per film, and the number of films you will honestly publish in two years. Then compare that figure with hiring per project, where flythroughs start from $1,250 and most single location projects land between $1,250 and $2,500 for a finished film with cutdowns, usage rights, and a delivery date in writing.

The pattern that emerges is consistent. Low frequency, high polish needs favor hiring. One or two marketing films a year will never repay the learning curve, and the hired version arrives finished, permitted, and ready to publish. High frequency, low polish needs favor buying. Weekly documentation repays ownership quickly, and nobody needs a delivery date in writing for an internal progress clip. The awkward middle, a few polished films a year, is where most businesses sit, and there a hybrid often wins: own a drone for the routine record, hire for the films your buyers will judge you by.

A simple test before you spend either way

Ask who on your team will own this work in month six, by name, and whether filming and editing sit anywhere in their actual job. If the answer is a name and a real slice of their week, buying has a future. If the answer is a shrug and a hope, the drone will join the shelf, and hiring per project is the honest spend.

Still torn? Describe your situation through the quote page and ask for the honest answer. We will do this same math with your numbers, and where buying wins, we will tell you to buy.

Consider also what each route does to your options next year. Hiring keeps your choices open: each project can use the right scope, the right season, and the right level of finish, and you can stop entirely without owning anything that reminds you of the decision. Buying commits you to a platform and a workflow, which is a strength when the work is steady and a weight when it is not. Businesses change faster than equipment pays for itself. The route that matches your honest forecast, rather than your hopeful one, is the route whose cost per published film will still look sensible when the year closes and the real numbers are counted.

There is a middle path some Omaha businesses use well. They rent or borrow nothing, guess at nothing, and instead hire one proper shoot to learn what good aerial work does for their numbers. That single result, measured in bookings or inquiries, tells them more about the buy versus hire question than any forecast, and the film itself keeps working while they decide about the following year.
