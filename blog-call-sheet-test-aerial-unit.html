The Call Sheet Test: What a Professional Aerial Unit Asks Before Shoot Day

You can judge an aerial unit before you see it fly. Listen to what it asks after receiving your call sheet. A professional asks about story, schedule, permissions, and post. An amateur asks only where to park and what time lunch is.

August 2026

Every production has felt the cost of a department that arrives unprepared. With aerial, that cost lands fast. Light moves, talent windows close, and a location question that should have been answered on Tuesday becomes a standoff on Friday. The call sheet test is a simple way to vet. Send the call sheet, the shot list, and the location. Then note the questions that come back. Their quality tells you how the shoot day will feel.

A strong unit reads the call sheet as a promise the whole production has made to itself. Call time, order of work, company moves, talent availability, client presence, and wrap target all shape what aerial can do. The unit should confirm where it fits in that order, what must happen before it flies, and what happens if the order slips. Silence on those points is not confidence. It is usually inexperience.

Questions about the story and the cut

Expect creative questions first. What job does each aerial shot do? What does the ground unit cover before and after it? What frame rate and profile does post need? Is there a reference the director trusts? A unit that thinks in sequences will ask about entry and out points, because footage that cuts is its product. A unit that only asks for a list of cool shots will deliver a folder your editor must rescue.

These are the same habits set out in our guide to briefing an aerial unit. Read the questions you receive against that guide. If the unit asks about title placement, client must avoid items, and standout take logging, it has worked for editors before. If it promises every shot on the board without asking about light or location, be careful.

Questions about schedule and the set

Next come logistics questions that protect your day. Who is the one contact who can approve a change on set? Where does the unit set up, charge, and hold? What radio channel carries go and hold calls? When are doors, elevators, or gates controlled, and by whom? What talent action must the flight sync with, and what is the reset time if a take fails?

  • Call and order. When aerial works, what precedes it, and what follows it.
  • Decision maker. One named contact for scope, safety, and schedule calls.
  • Sync points. Talent cues, vehicle moves, doors, and any timed location event.
  • Reset reality. Time to return, relaunch, and repeat a line cleanly.

In the Omaha metro, add travel and location rhythm to that list. A morning in Bellevue and an afternoon near the RiverFront are not one casual move. Traffic, load in, and a fresh location check at the second site all belong in the plan. A unit that knows the metro will raise that without being prompted.

Questions about permission, airspace, and weather

A professional asks early about the location check. Has the venue given permission for aerial work? Are there venue rules about where aircraft can launch and land? Does the site sit in airspace shaped by Eppley Airfield that needs advance planning? What is the crowd picture on the day? Those questions are process, and they should arrive while design changes are still cheap. Our approach to integration is summarised on our page for filmmakers, with airspace and venue rules checked during planning, not on the day.

Weather questions belong in the same group. What wind and rain limits will the unit work within? When is the weather call made, and who makes it with the producer? What is the backup order of shots if the calm window is short? On our work, the location and airspace check is free, replies come within one business day, and weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer. Hold any unit to written answers on these points, including us.

The honesty signal to listen for

The strongest signal is a unit that tells you something you did not want to hear, early and with a solution. This shot needs an earlier call. That line crosses a crowd area and should move. This half day list is really a full day list, because story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day, and compressing the work will not compress the light. A unit willing to risk the booking to protect the day is usually the unit that will protect your client on the day.

There is a limit we state about our own process too. If a production cannot yet provide a location, a date, or a decision maker, we can give a general view, but we cannot honestly promise feasibility. Every written quote lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and production files are delivered within two business days once the shoot happens. Promises made without the call sheet facts are guesses wearing a uniform. Use the test. Send the call sheet. Count the questions. The right unit will make your production feel more prepared before a single aircraft leaves its case.
