What Happens on Flythrough Day: Two Hours, One Flight Path, Zero Chaos

The fear is a production taking over your building: cables, crew, guests displaced, a day gone. A flythrough day looks nothing like that. It is closer to a well run site visit with one quiet aircraft, and most of its work is finished before the first flight begins.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Before the day: the route already exists

Flythrough day starts days earlier, with the free location and airspace check on your address, a conversation about what the film has to prove, and a route planned from your floor plan, photographs, or a short phone walkthrough you send us. By the time anyone arrives on site, the intended path is known: where the film opens, which doors it passes through, which rooms it lingers in, and where it ends.

That planning is why the day can be short. It also surfaces the decisions only you can make. Which room layout should be shown, a wedding setup, a boardroom, an ordinary trading day? Which spaces are out of bounds? Who holds doors and who is our contact if a surprise booking appears? A short call settles those, and the written quote you already hold lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, so the day itself carries no commercial surprises.

Venues across the metro plan differently. A downtown Omaha site near the RiverFront may need its exterior opening timed around foot traffic and airspace already checked in advance. A venue near Elkhorn or Gretna may need the outdoor portion timed for light across open ground and Nebraska wind at its calmest, usually earlier rather than later. The plan names those windows before the day, not during it.

The first half hour: walk, adjust, rehearse

On arrival, the first thing that happens is not a flight. It is a walk. We walk the planned route together at guest pace, checking it against the building as it stands that morning. Furniture moves by a person width, a door is propped or assigned a holder, a delivery that was due at ten is noted, a room that photographs dark turns out to carry the best light in the building. The route adjusts on foot, cheaply, before it adjusts in the air at greater cost.

Then the route is flown in practice, slowly, without polish as the goal. Practice flights reveal the tight turn, the mirror that catches the pilot, the glass door that confuses depth, the air current near a stairwell or entrance that nudges a small aircraft. Each gets a small fix: a different line, a slower entry, a door held at a wider angle. This rehearsal is the unglamorous core of the format, and it is what separates a smooth finished film from a novelty take.

  • Arrival and walk: route checked on foot with your contact.
  • Space reset: only what the route sees, nothing moved without your agreement.
  • Practice flights: slow passes to fix lines, light, doors, and tight points.
  • Final takes: the full route flown a small number of times, complete each time.
  • Review on site: the best take checked with you before anyone packs away.

The filming itself: quiet, short, and bounded

The aircraft is small, guarded, and flown at walking pace or slower through your interior. There is no lighting rig and no crew of six. Typically it is the pilot and your one contact in the building, with door holders borrowed for minutes at a time. Rooms in use are not entered. If a space fills unexpectedly, the route pauses or reorders, because a guest experience always outranks a shot list.

A complete final take lasts minutes. The time between takes is review and reset: a chair returned, a door closed and opened again, light changed as a cloud moves. Expect the whole visit to run about one to two hours for a single location, in line with the timings on our services page, and expect long stretches of that time to look like a conversation about furniture rather than a film set.

The comparison most venue teams find reassuring is in our flythrough, 360 tour, and regular video guide: a panoramic scan day involves equipment in every room for longer, and a full video production brings more people and more footprint. The flythrough is deliberately the lightest of the three inside your building.

When the day does not go to plan

Honesty belongs in a process article too. Sometimes the right call on the day is not to fly the full route: wind rises beyond what a smooth exterior opening can carry, a private event runs long into the filming window, a space is not as agreed and cannot be reset in time. When that happens we would rather reschedule the affected portion than hand you a wobbly, compromised film, and weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer. A flythrough is a promise about how your place feels. A rushed take breaks that promise in a way viewers can feel even if they cannot name it.

There is one more limit worth writing down. A single visit films the layout and condition agreed for that day. If you renovate, reconfigure the main room, or change the entrance, the film ages with the building, and a refilm of the changed section is a new conversation, not a free expectation. Knowing that in advance is part of buying the format with open eyes.

After the visit: what lands in your inbox

The edit begins from the best complete take, with colour, music, and pacing finished around it, plus short cutdowns for social use where the quote includes them. Finished films arrive within five business days, and we reply to messages within one business day throughout, so you are never guessing where your film is. You review, we adjust the small things a review exists for, and the master file is yours to place on the pages where your buyers decide.
