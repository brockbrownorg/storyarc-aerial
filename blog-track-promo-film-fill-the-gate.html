The Promo Film That Fills the Gate: What Tracks Should Shoot Before the Season Starts

Your season opener is six weeks away and the only footage you have to promote it with is last year's phone clips and a poster. Every track in the region has the same problem, and the ones that fill the gate early are the ones that solved it before the season started.

July 2026

A promo film is season infrastructure, not a post

Think of the promo film the way you think of the track surface: you prepare it before the season because everything else runs on it. One planned shoot before the opener can supply the season trailer, the vertical cuts that announce each round, the sponsor pitch film, and the background footage your own posts draw on for months. Shot early, it stops being content and starts being the raw material the whole year is marketed with.

The timing is the advantage. Before the season, the facility can be shown at its best and on a schedule you control: a test day, a practice day, or a planned session on the closed course, with organizer permission arranged calmly instead of requested in a panic. In this region that window is short. Nebraska and Iowa tracks move from winter quiet to weekly programs quickly in spring, and the calm weekends at places like I-29 Speedway in Pacific Junction or Motorsport Park Hastings disappear from the calendar once racing starts. The promo has to be planned in the quiet, or it will be chased all summer.

One rule frames the whole shoot and it is worth printing in the plan: sanctioned activity and closed courses with organizer permission only. A promo is filmed during approved activity, with the organizer's plan and the officials' limits in place, exactly like a race day. A promo that cuts corners on permission teaches everyone the wrong lesson before the season even opens.

What to shoot while you have the place to yourselves

  • The arrival. The road in, the gate, the grandstands, the view a first time fan gets. New spectators do not fear the racing, they fear not knowing what the place is like, and arrival footage answers that.
  • The track itself, whole. One aerial pass that lays out the full circuit or oval in a single view is the shot no ground camera can make, and it becomes the image your season is recognized by.
  • Speed, honestly framed. Chase and pass footage from approved running, planned with the drivers and riders involved, built up in speed rather than improvised.
  • The human day. Pits waking up, families arriving, food, shade, and the places a spectator actually spends an evening. Families buy the day, not only the racing.
  • The night finish. If your program runs under lights, film under lights. A promo that only shows daylight sells a product your fans will not recognize in August.

Our action and motorsports coverage is built for exactly this shape of work: one planned day that produces a main film and the cuts around it, scoped in a written quote that lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. Usage matters more for a promo than for almost anything else, because this film will run on your site, your social accounts, your sponsor decks, and possibly local advertising, and every one of those uses should be settled before the shoot, not after.

How one shoot becomes a season of material

Plan the cuts before you plan the flights. The main promo is the flagship, usually the piece your homepage and your sponsor meetings carry. Around it, the same footage becomes short vertical announcements for each round, a ticket focused cut that shows the spectator experience, a sponsor focused cut that shows banners, crowds, and hospitality, and quiet background clips your own team can post over all season without filming again.

The permission groundwork laid for the promo also serves the season. Officials who have briefed one calm, planned shoot with a crew understand exactly what that crew will ask for on a busy race day, and the crew understands the venue's limits from experience rather than from a document. That is the practical heart of how drone coverage gets permission at a race track: trust is built in the quiet days and spent on the loud ones.

What a promo film cannot do

Here is the part that costs us a sale now and then. A promo film cannot fill a gate by itself. If ticketing is confusing, if the schedule is posted late, if the spectator experience on the night is poor, a beautiful film will bring people once and the place itself will decide whether they return. We would rather tell a track that plainly than sell a film as a cure for problems a camera cannot fix.

It also cannot be built from nothing. A promo needs real activity to film: cars or bikes running, people in the pits, a day with some life in it. An empty track on a gray February morning in Nebraska, with wind cutting across open ground, will look like exactly that on film. If your pre season calendar has no practice or test activity to build around, the honest plan may be to shoot the promo across the first two rounds instead, and accept a later launch in exchange for footage that actually feels like your race nights. That tradeoff belongs in the conversation before anyone books a date.
