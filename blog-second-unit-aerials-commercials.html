Second Unit Aerials for Commercials: Small Crew, Big Establishing Shots

Your main unit is where the money is. Talent, product, client attention, and the performance that sells the spot all live there. Every hour that unit spends waiting for an establishing shot is an hour spent away from the work only it can do.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Second unit aerial exists to solve a scheduling truth. Commercials need place, scale, and transition, and they need them without pulling the director, the main camera, and the client away from talent for half a day. A small aerial unit, working from the same boards and the same call sheet discipline, captures the opening, the geography, and the closing image while the main unit keeps filming. When it works, the spot feels larger than its schedule. When it is treated as an afterthought, the aerial arrives in a different style and the editor must hide the join.

What second unit aerial does best

Establishing shots are the core assignment. A building in its Omaha setting, a venue at the hour when guests would arrive, a campus or plant that shows scale before the film moves inside. Transitions are the second assignment. A move across the RiverFront into downtown, a rise from a car park in Papillion to a full site view, or a closing pull back that returns the product to its place can carry the viewer between sections without a hard explanatory cut.

Second unit can also gather planned alternatives while the light is right. A dawn version and a late day version of the same opener give the editor and the client a choice in post, when the tone of the finished spot is clearer. That choice is cheap to capture when the unit is already positioned for the location, and expensive to recreate after the production wraps. The key word is planned. Alternatives belong on the shot list, with priority order, not as a vague request to get some options.

  • Openers and closers. Place and scale that frame the main unit story.
  • Transitions. Travel moves that connect locations and sections cleanly.
  • Light versions. Dawn and late day takes of the hero establishing shot.
  • Context coverage. Neighbourhood, approach roads, and setting the client wants recognised.

How it stays one production, not two

The risk is style drift, so integration must be deliberate. The aerial unit works from the director approved boards, the ground unit frame rate and profile, and a short check in at call and at wrap. Where the schedule allows, test frames are compared against main unit footage so colour and motion feel aligned. Communication runs through one producer contact, with any scope change approved there, exactly as described on our page for filmmakers.

The brief is the contract between units. It should name the story job of each aerial shot, the out point that meets main unit coverage, and the must avoid list for the location. Our guide to briefing an aerial unit gives the full pattern. A second unit with a weak brief will return beautiful general views. A second unit with a strong brief returns shots the editor was waiting for.

Scheduling and cost in plain terms

Second unit aerial is usually bought as a half day or full day block fitted around light and location access. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day. National production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 per day, and higher for larger productions. For a commercial already carrying crew, talent, and location costs, the relevant comparison is not the aerial line alone. It is the cost of tying up the main unit to chase geography it does not need to visit.

Delivery follows production discipline. Files are organised, labelled, and matched to the agreed settings, with a flight log that ties takes to the shot list. Production files are delivered within two business days. Every written quote lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and replies come within one business day during planning.

The honest limit of establishing work

We will say the part that argues against booking us for a full day without thought. A pile of handsome establishing shots does not save a spot with no idea. If the concept lives in a performance, a joke, or a product demonstration, aerial should support it briefly and get out of the way. Some commercials need one strong opener and one closer, captured inside a half day, and nothing more. Buying a full day of scenery because it feels cinematic can weaken the film and the budget at once.

Used with intent, second unit aerial is one of the best value lines in a commercial. It frees the main unit, protects the light windows, and gives post the geography that makes a place feel real. Omaha rewards it particularly well, because downtown, river, rail, and open metro locations sit close enough for one small unit to gather a full sense of place while your main crew stays exactly where the money is.

Give the second unit a wrap check as well. A two minute call to confirm standout takes, any missed item, and the delivery note means the main unit producer leaves the location knowing the establishing work is complete, rather than discovering a gap in the edit a week later.
