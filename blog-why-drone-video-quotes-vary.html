Why Three Drone Quotes for the Same Job Came Back at $600, $2,400, and $7,500

You sent the same short description to three operators and got back numbers that do not seem to belong to the same project. That gap feels like someone is overcharging, but usually it means each quote is quietly describing a different job, with different work, different deliverables, and different risk left on your side of the table.

November 2025

Start with what you actually asked for

Most quote requests sound simple. Film our venue. Cover our event. Get aerial shots of our building. Behind those few words sit a dozen decisions none of the operators can see from your message, so each one fills in the blanks with an assumption. One assumes you want clips to hand to an editor. Another assumes you want a finished film ready to post. A third assumes you want a full production with planning, crew, and a campaign edit. The totals diverge because the assumptions diverge, not because one operator typed an extra zero.

This is why comparing totals first is the trap. The right first question is not which number is fair. It is what job each number describes, line by line, and which of those jobs matches the outcome you need. Our full breakdown in what drone video actually costs in Omaha shows the ranges by project type, so you can see which family your project belongs in before you judge any single quote.

What the lowest quote usually includes, and leaves out

A quote near the bottom of the market usually covers arrival and flight time, and little else. Expect some version of this shape.

  • Raw or lightly trimmed clips. No finished edit, no music, no color work, no social cuts.
  • Permissions left to you. The airspace check and the venue permission may not be included, or may not happen at all.
  • No usage terms in writing. You have files, but nothing states where you may run them or for how long.
  • No weather plan. If Nebraska wind rises or a storm moves in, there is no agreed backup date and no stated policy.
  • No delivery date you can plan around. You get the files when you get them.

None of that makes the operator dishonest. For a buyer who has an editor, a simple location, and no deadline, that stripped down scope can be the right buy. Problems start when a buyer expects a finished marketing film and discovers, after the shoot, that they bought flight time only. The gap between those two expectations is where disappointment and extra cost live.

What the middle and top quotes are usually buying

The middle quote typically describes the finished product most businesses actually want. Planning comes first. The location and airspace check is handled. The flight path or shot list is agreed before anyone arrives. The deliverable is an edited film with music and color, plus short cutdowns for social use, a stated delivery date, and usage rights in writing. When we quote an FPV flythrough, most single location projects land between $1,250 and $2,500, and that is the shape of work inside that range.

The top quote is usually a different scale of job entirely. Think multiple locations, more than one shoot day, ground filming alongside aerial, interviews, a larger edit with several versions for different channels, or paid usage across a campaign. It may be exactly right for a launch or a season of content. It may also be more production than a single venue film needs. The honest point is that a high quote is not proof of greed and a low quote is not proof of value. Each has to be read against the work it lists.

Location alone can move a quote honestly. A wide open site outside Gretna is a simpler planning job than a downtown address near Eppley Airfield or a venue by the RiverFront, where airspace and permissions take real time to work through. If none of your three quotes even asked for the address, none of them has priced your actual location yet.

How to make the three quotes answer the same questions

Send every operator the same short list and ask for the answers in writing.

  • What exactly do I receive, and in what formats?
  • Is the edit included, and how many versions?
  • Who handles the airspace check and venue permission?
  • What usage rights are included, where, and for how long?
  • What is the delivery date, and what happens if weather moves the shoot?
  • What is not included that I might assume is?

When the answers come back in the same shape, the totals finally mean something. You may still choose the cheapest scope, but you will be choosing it with open eyes. You may also find the middle quote is the cheapest route to the finished outcome, because the low quote would need an editor, extra revisions, and rights sorted out later, each with its own bill.
