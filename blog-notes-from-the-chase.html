Notes From the Chase: What Filming Speed Teaches and Demands

I want to be plain about what this essay is. It is not a memoir of a paid season at the tracks, because I have not done that work and this site does not print experience that has not happened. It is a set of craft notes on what chase filming teaches and demands, learned from years of fast FPV flying, close study of sanctioned racing, and respect for the people who do this at full speed.

November 2026

First, the honest frame

Chase filming has a mythology problem. Online, it looks like pure nerve: a drone glued to a car or a bike, speed answering speed, the pilot as a kind of airborne daredevil. That picture sells clips. It also hides everything that makes the work legitimate, which is preparation, permission, and restraint. I would rather write about the hidden part, because the hidden part is where the craft lives.

My grounding for these notes is specific and I will keep it that way. Years of FPV flying around Omaha and the Midwest. Freestyle craft flown fast in open country until speed stopped feeling like chaos and started feeling like information. Cinewhoops flown slowly in tight spaces, which taught the other half of the lesson, control at the pace of a walk. Long hours studying how sanctioned racing is filmed on closed courses, and editing my own fast footage to see where it actually failed. That is the experience underneath this essay. A season of paid track work is not in it, yet, and any sentence here that sounds like it is should be read as a standard I am holding myself to, not a credit I am claiming.

The standards themselves are not speculation. Across Nebraska and Iowa, legitimate chase work happens at sanctioned events and on closed courses, with the organizer's permission, or it does not happen at all. I write about how coverage is properly priced and scoped in my guide to what race day aerial coverage costs, but no price matters until that permission exists. The chase begins with a yes from the people responsible for the course. Everything else is commentary.

Keeping up is the least of it

The first lesson the chase teaches is that speed is a reading problem before it is a flying problem. A car on a course or a rider on a track is following a line, and the line has a logic: braking points, turn ins, places where the machine is settled and places where it is busy surviving. A pilot who only reacts to the machine will always be late, and lateness at speed looks like panic. A pilot who reads the line can be early, positioned where the action is about to be, moving at a pace the viewer can follow.

Freestyle flying taught me the physical half of this. At speed, your inputs have to shrink as your anticipation grows. Big corrections are just confessions that you misread something seconds ago. Editing fast footage taught me the visible half. Viewers do not experience raw pace the way a pilot does. They experience relationships: the machine growing in frame, the background starting to run, the moment the drone yields the lead and lets speed pass through the shot. A chase that never varies that relationship becomes a screensaver at ninety miles an hour. Fast and boring is a real outcome, and only reading prevents it.

Here is the claim I expect racers and pilots alike to argue with. The best chase pilot in a given moment is often the one who chooses not to chase. A wider, steadier shot that shows the machine working through a whole section of course tells a truer story of speed than a tight pursuit that shows nothing but commitment. Proximity is a seasoning. Judgment is the meal.

Restraint is a safety practice and an aesthetic one

Every serious conversation about filming speed returns to margins. Margin from the machine, margin from people, margin for the mistake nobody planned. In practice those margins are set before the day begins, in the conversation with the organizer about where a drone may be, where it may never be, and what ends a flight instantly. The pilot who treats those boundaries as creative restrictions has misunderstood the assignment. The boundaries are the assignment. Inside them, be brilliant. Outside them, be on the ground.

What surprises newcomers is that restraint also reads better on screen. A drone holding a disciplined distance lets the machine own the frame, and the machine is the subject the audience paid to see. A drone crowding the action makes itself the subject, and chase footage where the camera becomes the story has failed in the same way a flythrough fails when the flying upstages the building. The action lane described on our services page is built on that order of loyalties. Subject first, aircraft last, every time.

The honest cost belongs here too. Plenty of classic chase shots are simply unavailable to a pilot working responsibly, and I would rather name that loss than chase the footage anyway. The low pass over a crowded section, the threading run between a machine and its audience, the shot that requires a crowd underneath the flight path. Those shots exist online. Their existence is not an argument. A pilot building a real practice in this region has to accept a smaller menu than the internet shows and cook it better.

What a season of this work would demand

I think often about what a full season of sanctioned chase work across Nebraska and Iowa would demand of a pilot, because naming the demands is how you prepare for them honestly. It would demand a body of craft maintained to a standard that survives tired Sundays. Batteries, airframes, and judgment all managed like a racer manages a machine. It would demand relationships with organizers built slowly, on reliability rather than reels. It would demand an editor's patience after every event, turning hours of speed into minutes that respect what the day actually was.

Most of all, it would demand the discipline these notes keep circling: read the line, hold the margin, serve the subject, and let the unverifiable shot go. Those are learnable things. I know, because the learning is the part I have actually done, in fields and in edit bays and in the long study of people who film speed well. When a real season of track work comes, it will be written about here in the past tense, with names the organizers allow and lessons the work itself taught. Until then, these notes are the truest thing I can offer on the subject, and I would rather offer a true thing than a taller one.
