The Midwest Is a Better Classroom Than the Coasts: A Regional Filmmaker's Case

Filmmakers on the coasts learn their craft surrounded by beauty, crew, and industry. I learned mine in Nebraska wind with no one to call. I would not trade educations, and I think the harder classroom produces the more resourceful filmmaker.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The education nobody advertises

There is a standard story about where filmmakers are made, and Omaha is not in it. The story says you move to where the industry lives, assist people who know more than you, and absorb the craft by proximity. I do not doubt that story works for many people. I doubt it is the only education, and I have come to believe it is not even the most rigorous one.

My classroom has been the Midwest. I learned FPV flying in Nebraska and Iowa, in fields and small towns and around Omaha, through years of freestyle and cinematic practice, whoops in tight spaces and bigger craft in open country. I learned videography by shooting and editing my own work because there was no department to hand it to. I learned storytelling by studying films at night and testing what I had learned the next morning, alone, in weather that frequently voted against the lesson.

Here is the claim my coastal peers are welcome to argue with. Constraint teaches judgment faster than abundance does. A filmmaker surrounded by dramatic coastline, golden hills, and a deep bench of specialists can make handsome work for years without ever discovering whether they actually have an eye. The environment does too much of the work. The Midwest refuses to do the work for you. Every good frame here is earned, and the earning is the education.

Wind, light, and the long drive

Start with the wind, because every Nebraska pilot does. Wind here is not an occasional condition. It is a standing collaborator with strong opinions. It has taught me to read a forecast like a contract, to build flights that degrade gracefully when the air turns rough, and to know the difference between a shot that wants calm and a shot that can use motion. Pilots in gentler climates can postpone that education for years. Mine arrived in the first season and has never left.

The light teaches a parallel lesson. Much of the Midwest year offers flat, honest light that flatters nothing. There are no canyon walls bouncing gold into every scene. To make a compelling image under a gray November sky in Iowa, you have to understand composition, movement, and timing at a level that beautiful locations let you fake. When the great light does come, and it does, in June evenings over the Missouri River and October afternoons when the harvest turns every field edge amber, a Midwest filmmaker recognizes it instantly. Scarcity trains gratitude, and gratitude trains attention.

Distance is the third teacher. Work and subjects here are spread across real geography. A filmmaker based in Omaha learns to plan a whole day around one location, to arrive self sufficient, and to solve problems with what the vehicle holds. That is also, not incidentally, an economic education. I write honestly about what regional work costs and why in my guide to what a brand film costs in the Midwest. Out here, nobody can bill a client for the inefficiencies a big market absorbs. You learn economy because your clients are neighbors who will notice.

Doing every job yourself

The least glamorous advantage of a regional practice is that there is no one to delegate your weaknesses to. I fly the aircraft, plan the flight, shoot the ground footage when a story needs it, edit the result, color it, sound design it, and deliver it. Each of those jobs has exposed ignorance the others depended on. Editing taught me to fly for the cut. Flying taught me to edit with restraint. Planning taught me both.

Coastal specialization produces masters, and I do not dismiss that. But it also produces filmmakers who have never felt the whole weight of a finished piece, and that weight is instructive in a way no single department can replicate. When a regional filmmaker's film fails, there is nowhere to look but the mirror. I have had those evenings with a timeline full of my own footage and no one to blame for the gaps in it. Those evenings built more judgment than any success I have had.

This is also why I believe the region is an honest partner for visiting productions, a case I make practically on our page for filmmakers. A crew that hires Midwest support is not hiring a discount version of a coastal unit. They are hiring people whose education included every job, every weather, and every consequence.

There is one more subject in the curriculum, and it may be the most useful of all: an audience that knows you. In Omaha, the people who watch your work are often the people who watched you learn it. There is no anonymity to hide behind and no vast market to absorb a careless job. Reputation here travels at the speed of a small city, which is to say instantly and permanently. That pressure is uncomfortable. It is also a superb teacher of thoroughness, because every shortcut knows it will be recognized.

What the classroom cannot give

The honest limit, stated plainly. The Midwest will not hand a filmmaker the network, the volume, or the budgets that the industry centers hold, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Some careers genuinely require leaving, and some kinds of work simply do not happen here at scale. I have no quarrel with anyone who goes. My quarrel is only with the assumption that staying is the lesser education.

Staying is a different education. Slower in contacts, faster in judgment. Thinner in glamour, thicker in resourcefulness. The coasts will always make filmmakers who know how the industry works. The Midwest makes filmmakers who know how the work works, because the work was the only teacher available. Given the choice between a classroom with a view and a classroom with a standard, I will take the standard every time. The wind is still teaching. I am still enrolled.

Brock Brown is the founder and pilot at StoryArc Aerial in Omaha, Nebraska. He flies cinematic FPV and aerial video for venues, brands, events, race days, and visiting production crews, and writes these guides the way StoryArc quotes: in plain English, with the numbers showing.
