How to Brief a Videographer So the Quote Comes Back Right

Vague inquiries get vague quotes, and vague quotes are where surprise fees are born. The fix is not a long document. A good brief fits in a short message, and it contains five things that let a videographer price your actual project instead of a generic one. Here they are, with what each one changes.

January 2026

The five parts of a brief that works

  • The outcome. What should this film cause? More tours, more ticket sales, a sponsor renewed, a homepage that finally converts. One or two sentences. The outcome decides the shape of the film more than any camera choice, and it tells the operator what success means when tradeoffs appear.
  • The place. The address, and what kind of space it is. Location decides the airspace and permission work, the light, and the travel. A barn venue outside Gretna and a rooftop in the Old Market near Eppley Airfield are different planning jobs at the same headline idea, and a quote written without the address is a quote written about neither.
  • The date, and how fixed it is. An event date that cannot move is a different commitment from a venue shoot that can slide a week for better weather. Say which kind yours is, and say the date the finished film is needed, which is the date the quote will be built backward from.
  • The audience and where the film will run. Homepage, booking page, social feeds, sponsor reports, paid ads. This sets the versions needed, the shapes they must fit, and the usage rights the quote must include.
  • What you already have and who else is involved. An editor waiting for raw files, a photographer booked the same day, a brand guide with colors and fonts, a decision maker who must approve the plan. Each fact removes a guess, and guesses are what operators pad prices against.

That is the whole brief. Notice what is absent: camera language, shot lists, technical wishes. You are the expert on your business and your outcome. The operator is the expert on how to film it. A brief that respects that split gets a sharper quote than a brief that tries to direct from a distance. What a good brief does to the price

It lowers the risk premium hiding inside it. Every unknown in a project is a place where an operator must either pad the price or gamble. Padded prices protect the operator and cost you money. Gambled prices win jobs and produce disputes. A brief that closes the big unknowns, place, date, deliverables, usage, lets an honest operator quote the work itself, tightly, because there is less fog to insure against.

It also makes quotes comparable. Send the same brief to three operators and the replies finally describe the same job, which turns a confusing spread of totals into a readable comparison of scope, deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. Send three different improvisations and you will receive three different projects wearing price tags, and no checklist can rescue the comparison afterward.

Two small additions that punch above their weight

First, name your budget band if you have one. A range you can truly spend lets the operator design the best scope inside it and show you the tradeoffs openly, instead of quoting the complete version and losing a workable project. Second, name the thing you are worried about. Wind on an exposed site in Bellevue, crowds at a public event, a space that photographs smaller than it feels, a tight turnaround before a seasonal opening. Worries named early become planning items. Worries discovered on the day become problems, and problems are the most expensive line item in any production.

The honesty note: a good brief occasionally reveals that the project should be smaller, later, or handled another way entirely. A brief for a film to rescue a booking page that is losing people for other reasons describes a website problem with a video budget attached. When we see that shape, we will say so, even though saying so ends the quote conversation.

Send your brief through the quote page in whatever form you have it, rough edges included. A rough brief with the five parts present beats a polished message missing the address, every time.

If writing even a short brief feels like one task too many in a full week, use the spoken version. Call or record a voice note and answer the five parts aloud, in any order, in your own words. Outcome, place, date, audience, and who else is involved can all be spoken in about ninety seconds, and a good operator will turn that into a written brief and send it back for your correction before quoting. What matters is that the information moves from your head, where it is complete, into the shared record, where a price can be built on it. The format has always mattered less than the completeness.

Finally, send the brief before you feel fully ready. A brief sent at eighty percent complete starts a conversation that finishes the remaining twenty percent quickly, often in a single reply asking the two or three questions that actually matter. A brief held for another month of polishing delays the quote, the booking window, and the season you hoped to film in, all to perfect a document whose whole job is to start a conversation.
