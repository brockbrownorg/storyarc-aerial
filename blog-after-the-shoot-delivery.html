What a Production Should Expect in the Two Days After the Shoot

Wrap is not delivery. The hours after the shoot decide whether your editor starts cutting on day two or spends it renaming files and guessing which take the director liked. A professional unit treats those two days as part of the shoot, not an errand after it.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

You should know, before the shoot day, exactly what will happen after it. Who holds the only copy of the footage, and for how long? When are selects made? What notes travel with the files? When does your editor receive a drive or a link that can be opened and used without a follow up call? For production work, our standard is clear. Production files are delivered within two business days of the shoot day, organised, labelled, and matched to the settings agreed in the creative call. This article walks through what fills those two days.

Day zero is still part of delivery

The work starts before anyone leaves the location. Takes are logged on set, with locations, times, and standout takes noted while memory is fresh. Cards are handled as the only record of the day until a backup exists. At wrap, the producer should hear a plain summary. What was captured as planned, what changed, and anything post should know before the files arrive. If a shot was redesigned because of wind or a venue condition, that fact belongs in the wrap conversation, not buried in a folder name.

Back in the studio, the first task is protection. Files are copied and verified before any selection or colour work begins. That discipline sounds dull until the day it saves a production. A unit that cannot describe its backup step in one sentence is asking you to carry a risk you did not agree to. Ask about it on the consult call. The question is part of the planning set out on our page for filmmakers.

Day one: ingest, selects, and honest notes

Day one is for ingest and review. Files are organised into the structure your editor requested, with naming that matches the brief where one was provided. Selects are marked where that service is part of the order. Shots captured in a flat profile are left ready for your grade, with settings stated in the delivery note. Any clip with a known issue, such as a light change mid move or a brief obstruction at the edge of frame, is flagged rather than quietly included as if it were clean.

This is also where the flight log earns its place. It connects the file names to the call sheet, so an editor who was not on set can find the approved take quickly. The habits that make this possible start earlier, in our guide to briefing an aerial unit. A brief that named purpose per shot gives day one a map. Without it, selection becomes taste, and taste takes longer.

  • Organised structure. Folders and names that drop into your project without renaming.
  • Delivery note. Profile, settings, standout takes, and any flagged clips in plain words.
  • Flight log. Locations, times, and the takes the set approved.
  • Clear scope. Raw clips, curated selects, or a finished sequence, as the quote listed.

Day two: delivery your editor can start on

By the end of day two, the production receives the files as promised. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, so delivery day is a check against writing, not a negotiation. Confirm receipt with your editor, not just with production. The editor is the person who can say whether the files open, match, and cut. If a naming or profile problem appears, it should be raised while the shoot is still fresh and the unit can correct the handover quickly.

Separate production files from finished films in your planning. A finished film from a direct client shoot follows its own edit timeline, while production files are built for your post team and move within two business days. Confusing the two creates false urgency. If your edit bay is on a hard deadline and needs same day selects for a client review, name that need on the creative call. It can often be built into the day. Promising it after wrap, when the schedule was set for standard delivery, is where trust gets damaged.

What can honestly slow delivery

We will name the limits. A very large shoot day with many locations across Omaha, from the RiverFront to a late call in Elkhorn, produces more material to verify and organise. A request to cut a finished aerial sequence adds edit time beyond file delivery. A client change to naming or delivery destination after wrap can add a step. None of these should become a surprise. If any of them applies, you hear about the timing before the shoot, in the quote or the feasibility notes.

Replies during this window come within one business day as standard, and faster communication is possible when the production names an urgent post need early. The goal is simple. Two days after the shoot, your editor is cutting, your producer has a delivery note that matches the quote, and no one is hunting through a drive wondering what they are looking at. That is what a production buys when it hires a unit that thinks past the flight.
