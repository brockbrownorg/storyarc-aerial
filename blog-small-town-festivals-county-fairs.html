Small Town Festivals and County Fairs: Big Energy on a Small Budget

Your festival runs on a small board, local sponsors, and volunteers who also have day jobs. You cannot buy coverage like a city event. You may not need to. A focused plan can capture the scale and joy that bring people back next year.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

What small events actually need from film

A county fair or a small town festival has one marketing problem a city event does not. People outside your county may have no picture of it at all, and people inside it assume they already know it. A short film that shows the whole grounds alive, the midway or main street full, the grandstand at dusk, changes both minds at once.

You do not need a library of cuts to do that. Most small events need three things.

  • One strong recap film of one to two minutes for social, the local paper, and next year promotion.
  • A handful of clean still life moments in motion: the grounds whole, the parade route, the evening lights, the place your town is proudest of, seen from the air.
  • A sponsor thank you cut or section that shows the local bank, dealer, and co op names working in front of the neighbours who funded them.

That is a half day of well aimed work in many cases, because event coverage is priced by the half day or full day and a fair programme usually has one clear peak: parade morning, the big grandstand night, or the final evening when lights and crowds are at their best. Buy the peak, not the whole weekend.

Ways a small budget goes further

Share the visit. If two nearby events or a town and its school or chamber need filming in the same season, one trip planned for both can reduce travel and setup cost for each. Ask openly; small towns are good at this kind of practical cooperation.

Use your volunteers for ground colour. Phones in many hands will capture faces and small moments all weekend. A professional visit can then concentrate on what volunteers cannot make: the aerial scale, the opening and closing views, and the edit that turns a folder of clips into a film. Agree in advance if you want outside footage included, because quality and rights need a quick check.

Film the empty grounds too. A short session while the fair is being set up, rides in place and banners up with nobody in the way, is calm, crowd safe, and gives your film its establishing views without competing with the busiest hours.

Rural Nebraska helps you here. Open ground around a fairgrounds outside towns like those in Sarpy, Douglas, and Washington counties gives clear launch areas and long views, and the airspace picture is often simpler than downtown Omaha near Eppley Airfield. Simpler is not automatic, though. The location and airspace check is still free and still worth doing, and venue or fair board permission belongs in writing.

Crowd practice at a fair, stated plainly

Fairs gather everyone: toddlers, grandparents, livestock, and crowds that shift all day. Our practice is restraint. We do not fly over guests, midway crowds, or grandstand seating. We launch from clear ground apart from the public, film gatherings from the perimeter and at a distance, and lean on the quiet hours and the empty grounds for the closest aerial work.

Animals deserve a specific mention at a county fair. Tell us where livestock is housed and shown, and expect us to keep distance and to pause if animals are unsettled. A good fair film never comes at the cost of the animals or the kids showing them.

The honest answer for the smallest events

Now the part a small board deserves to hear before spending. If your event is free, tiny, and carried entirely by the same families who attend it, professional aerial film may not return its cost, and we will tell you that when you ask for the honest answer. A borrowed drone flight by a trusted local hobbyist for personal memories, where rules and permission allow, or a single ground videographer for the parade, may serve you better this year.

The case turns when you need to grow beyond the faithful, renew sponsors who are choosing among many local asks, or bid for visitors from Omaha, Papillion, and Bellevue who plan summer weekends in advance. Then the logic in your recap video is next year's ticket sales team applies in small town form. Next year's crowd is deciding from a phone screen, and the fair that can show its scale and its lights will win Saturdays from the fair that can only describe them.

Start with the peak half day, one recap, one sponsor section, and the empty grounds at good light. If the film earns, and for a growing fair it often can, you will know exactly what a fuller day would add next year, because you will have a year of real use to judge it by.
