10 Questions to Ask Any Drone Videographer Before You Book (Including Us)

Hiring a drone videographer feels risky mostly because you cannot see the work behind the work until something goes wrong. These ten questions surface that hidden work before you book, and because a checklist is easy to publish and harder to live under, our own answers are printed beside every question.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The questions, with our answers beside them

1. Who checks the airspace and gets permission for my location? Our answer: we do. The location and airspace check is free, and it happens before a quote is final. A venue in Elkhorn and a venue downtown near Eppley Airfield or the RiverFront raise different questions, and we work through the permissions your specific address needs rather than assuming them.

2. What exactly do I receive at the end? Our answer: whatever the written quote lists, named one by one. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. If a film, a cutdown, or a set of files is not on that list, it is not part of the job, and we would rather you know that before the shoot than after it.

3. Is the edit included, or am I buying raw footage? Our answer: it depends on the product you choose, and the quote says which. Most flythrough and brand work we quote is a finished film with music and color. Raw files for your own editor are a separate choice, priced and named as their own line when you want them.

4. What usage rights do I get, in writing? Our answer: the quote states where the film may run and for how long. That clause is not fine print to us. It is one of the three things every quote must carry, alongside deliverables and the delivery date, and we are glad to walk through it before you sign anything. The background thinking is laid out in our cost guide for Omaha buyers.

5. What happens if the weather turns? Our answer: weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer. Nebraska wind and fast moving summer storms are a normal part of planning here, so we watch the forecast with you, make the call early, and move the shoot without turning the weather into a fee.

Five more questions, same treatment

6. How long will you be on site, and how much disruption should we expect? Our answer: most single location flythroughs film in one to two hours, with the flight path planned before we arrive. Event coverage is booked by the half day or full day. We plan around your working day rather than asking your day to stop for us, and the quote states the time block you are buying.

7. When will I have the finished film? Our answer: finished films within five business days, and production files within two business days, with the actual delivery date written into your quote. If your date is tighter than that, say so before booking, because rush delivery changes the price and should never arrive as a surprise.

8. How many revisions are included? Our answer: the quote names it. Revisions are a normal part of finishing a film, and the included rounds belong in writing, along with what an extra round costs. If another operator will not state a number, treat that silence as an answer.

9. Can I see full length work like my project, not only a reel? Our answer: ask us, and ask everyone. A short reel shows best seconds. A full film shows pacing, sound, and whether the work holds together for the length your buyers will watch. We will point you to the closest full examples we can share for your project type, and where we have no close match, we will say so instead of dressing up a distant one.

10. What would make you tell me not to book this? Our answer: if your customers do not choose you by seeing your place, if the film would run once and vanish, or if your space cannot be flown in the way the idea needs, booking us is the wrong spend. That answer costs us work on a regular basis, and it stays on this page anyway, because a business that hears no from us once tends to trust the yes later.

How to use this list with anyone

Send the ten questions to every operator on your short list and compare the written answers side by side. Vague answers are information. An operator who answers plainly, in writing, before money moves, is showing you how they will behave when the shoot day gets complicated. An operator who resists writing things down is also showing you.

When you are ready, put us through the same test through the quote page. Ask for our answers in writing, hold them against this page, and hold us to any place where the two do not match.

Notice what these questions have in common. None of them asks about the drone. Equipment matters, but it is the easiest part of this work to buy and the fastest part to become outdated. The questions instead probe judgment, process, and honesty under ordinary pressure: a difficult location, a moving forecast, a client whose real need is smaller than the sale on offer. An operator who handles those moments well will handle your shoot day well. An operator who dazzles on equipment and goes vague on process has told you where their strength ends, and it ends before the part you are actually hiring.
