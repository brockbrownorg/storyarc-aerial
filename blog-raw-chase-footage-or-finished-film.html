Raw Chase Footage or Finished Film? The Fork Every Team Faces

The quote question sounds simple, raw files or a finished film, but teams choose wrong in both directions: paying for an edit they would rather control, or taking raw files that sit unedited while the season moves on without them. The right answer depends on one thing most teams do not examine until it is too late.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The fork is really a question about your editor

Raw chase footage is a professional product for a team that has editing capacity: a media person, a capable sponsor contact, or a production partner who will take files and return films. Finished film is the product for a team whose capacity is racing. Everything else, price, speed, and control, flows from that single fact, so answer it honestly before you compare any numbers.

Be specific about what capacity means. An editor is not a teammate who owns editing software. It is someone who will actually open the files during a race week, when the car or bike also needs work, and deliver cuts while they still matter. Many teams discover in May that their editor was a winter idea. A season of raw files nobody touched is the most expensive coverage a team can buy, because it costs real money and produces nothing a sponsor ever saw.

Whichever side of the fork you choose, the filming conditions do not change. We fly sanctioned events and closed courses with organizer permission only, briefed with the organizer and coordinated on the day. Raw delivery does not mean informal flying, and a team buying files should ask any crew the same permission questions covered in how drone coverage gets permission at a race track. Files obtained the wrong way are a liability in a sponsor's hands, not an asset.

What raw actually means when it is done properly

  • Organized files, not a memory card. Sessions and runs identified clearly enough that an editor who was not there can find the right pass in minutes.
  • Settings your editor can work with. Frame rate, profile, and format agreed before the shoot, matched to your other cameras where that matters, so aerial cuts into ground footage without a fight.
  • Fast delivery. Production files from us arrive within two business days, because raw footage has a short useful life for a team posting during a season.
  • Rights in writing. Usage rights stated in the quote, including sponsor use, so your editor is never building a film on footage whose terms are unclear.

Raw is where our action and motorsports coverage meets the production world most directly. For productions and teams with editors, day block thinking applies: story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day, and national production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 and up per day. Those published figures describe the filming day. The edit you are not buying is the saving, and the work you are taking on is the cost, and both sides of that trade should be visible before you sign.

What a finished film buys that raw cannot

A finished film buys certainty of outcome. Structure, music, color, captions, sponsor placement, and vertical versions all arrive done, on a stated date: finished films from us arrive within five business days. For a team whose sponsor deliverables are contractual, that certainty is not a luxury. A sponsor report cannot be built on footage that might be edited soon.

It also buys an outside eye. A crew that films and edits sees the story of your day differently from the people who lived it, and will usually lead with the moment that explains your team to a stranger rather than the moment that felt biggest in the pits. For sponsor and recruiting material, the stranger's view is the one that pays. Raw footage handed to an insider editor tends to produce films for the team. Finished films tend to produce films for the audience. Know which audience each piece is for.

There is a middle shape worth naming, because many regional teams land there: raw files for your own fast social posting, plus one finished film per round or per season for sponsors and major announcements. It costs more than raw alone and less than finishing everything, and it matches how teams actually publish. If that shape fits, say so at the quote stage, because the filming plan and the file organization change when an edit is expected later.

How to decide before the season starts

Run one test before committing a season. Take the files from one covered day and watch what actually happens to them in the following two weeks. If cuts appear, credited, posted, and sent to sponsors, you have an editor and raw is a sound buy. If the files wait while the team prepares for the next round, you do not have an editor this season, whatever the plan said, and finished film is the cheaper product in every way that matters.

And the limit from our side, plainly: we will sell raw footage happily to a team set up to use it, and we will also tell a team without an editor that raw is the wrong purchase, even though it is an easier sale for us. A hard drive of unedited chase footage has never renewed a sponsor. Choose the fork that ends in films people see, and put the choice, the delivery dates, and the usage rights in the written quote before the first round, so the season runs on a decision instead of a hope.
