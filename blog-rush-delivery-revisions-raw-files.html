Rush Delivery, Revisions, and Raw Files: The Add Ons, Explained

Almost every unhappy video purchase traces back to one of three small items that nobody named at the start: the film was needed faster than standard, the revisions ran past the included rounds, or the raw files turned out to be a separate product. None of the three is unreasonable. All three are far cheaper to settle before the shoot than after it.

January 2026

Add on one: rush delivery

Standard delivery exists for a reason. Editing well takes unhurried hours: selecting from many passes, building the shape, coloring, placing music, making the cutdowns, and reviewing the whole with fresh eyes. Our standard is finished films within five business days and production files within two business days, and most launches can be planned around that rhythm comfortably.

Rush delivery compresses the same work into evenings and immediate turnaround. For an event film, it can be worth every cent. A recap published while the event is still the talk of the week, during College World Series time in Omaha for example, does work that the same film cannot do nine days later. For a venue film with no fixed launch date, rush is usually money spent to relieve a feeling rather than to gain an outcome. We say that knowing it talks buyers out of a fee we could charge: if nothing real is gained by day two that day five would lose, take the standard window and keep the money.

Settle it at the quote stage, where rush is a priced option with a stated delivery hour, not at the delivery stage, where it becomes a plea.

Add on two: revisions

Revisions are a healthy part of finishing a film. You know your business in ways the edit cannot, and a round of changes, swapping a shot, adjusting a title, trimming a section, is how a good film becomes the right film. A professional quote names how many rounds are included and what further rounds cost. That naming protects both sides: you from open ended fees, the editor from open ended rounds.

Most revision trouble is really briefing trouble. Late changes to the goal of the film, a new audience, a different offer, a stakeholder seeing the project for the first time at the edit stage, are not revisions in the small sense. They are a new scope wearing revision clothing, and they will be priced like one by any operator. The defense is simple and lives before the shoot: agree the goal, the audience, and the approvers in writing, and show the near final plan to everyone whose opinion can veto it. The ranges in our Omaha cost guide assume that normal shape of work, agreed scope plus named revision rounds.

Add on three: raw files

Raw files are the camera original material, delivered for editing by someone else. They are a legitimate product for productions, agencies, and businesses with a real editor and a real plan for the material. They are an add on for a simple reason: preparing, organizing, and delivering large original files is separate work, and the usage conversation around raw material is wider, because whoever holds the raw files can make future films from them indefinitely.

Buy raw files when a future use exists that you can name. A season of cuts an in house editor will build, a production library, an agency campaign with versions still unwritten. Do not buy them as vague insurance. The common fate of insurance raw files is an unopened drive, and the honest price of that insurance is rarely worth paying. If you do buy them, get the format, the delivery window, and the selection scope, usable passes or everything recorded, stated in the quote, along with how long the download will remain available.

The habit that makes all three boring

Every surprise in this article shares one cure: name it in the written quote. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and the add ons belong on the same page, each with a price or an explicit note that it is not included. A buyer in Papillion or Elkhorn planning a calm launch can then choose standard timing, included revisions, and no raw files with confidence, while an event organizer facing a fixed date can buy the rush window deliberately, in daylight, at a known price.

When you request a quote through the quote page, tell us any of the three you might need, even tentatively. A tentative mention gets an option price. An unmentioned need gets a negotiation later, and later is always the more expensive room to negotiate in.

The same logic applies to timing your request. Add ons named at the quote stage join an orderly queue of work. The rush fee buys planned evening hours, the revision rounds are scheduled into the edit, and the raw files are prepared while the project material is still open on the system. The same requests made after delivery reopen finished work, and reopening anything costs more than including it the first time, in editing as in building. Early honesty about what you might need is not a commitment to buy it. It is simply the cheapest moment to learn its price and decide with the full picture in front of you.
