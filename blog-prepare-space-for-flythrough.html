Preparing Your Space for a Flythrough: The One Hour Checklist

Your venue does not need a deep clean, a renovation, or a styled photoshoot to film well. It needs one focused hour, spent in the order the camera will travel, on the small things a continuous route notices and a photograph never would.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Why preparation looks different for a flythrough

A still photographer can crop around a stacked chair, a cleaning trolley, or a cable across the floor. A flythrough cannot. The camera travels the route your guests travel, at their height, in one unbroken movement, so anything untidy along that line appears in the film exactly as a guest would meet it. That sounds demanding. In practice it makes preparation simpler, because you only need to perfect one line through the building, not every corner of it.

Work in route order. Start where the film starts, usually outside, and walk slowly towards where it ends, seeing the building the way the lens will. Anything you fix out of order tends to get missed, because the route is a sequence of reveals and each reveal deserves its own ten seconds of attention.

One person should own this hour: your contact for the shoot, with authority to move furniture and make small calls. Committees prepare badly. The one hour checklist, in route order

  • Outside first, ten minutes. Move bins, hoses, and stray cones out of the arrival line. Check the entrance looks like an entrance: door clean, signage visible, nothing stored against it.
  • The threshold, five minutes. Clear the doorway swing completely. The camera passes through it, so anything behind the door is in the film and in the flight path.
  • The main space, twenty minutes. Set the layout you actually sell, wedding, boardroom, dining, or open floor, and set it completely. Half dressed tables read worse than plain ones. Align chairs. Straighten what a guest eye would catch in a slow pass.
  • The route edges, ten minutes. Walk the sides of the route, not the centre. Cleaning supplies, stacked furniture, spare stock, and cables live at the edges, and the camera sees the edges.
  • Light, ten minutes. Open blinds where daylight helps, replace dead bulbs, set evening lighting if the film is a dusk piece. Continuous film shows mixed, patchy light honestly, so make it deliberate.
  • People and doors, five minutes. Agree who holds which door, who is in frame by design, and which rooms stay closed. Brief anyone on site that a quiet aircraft will pass through.

What to leave alone

Resist the urge to redecorate. A flythrough sells your real space to people who will then visit it, and a room dressed beyond what a guest would meet sets a trap for your own team at the tour stage. Leave the lived in details that carry character: the worn bar top with a story, the noticeboard, the garden as it honestly grows. Remove clutter, not personality.

Also leave temporary promotions out of the main route where you can. Banners, seasonal offers, and dated displays age a film faster than anything else in frame. A flythrough should be usable for seasons. If a display matters commercially, place it where a cutdown can feature it, so the master film stays clean when the promotion ends.

Weather preparation is mostly a decision, not a task. Decide in advance what happens to the outdoor portion if Nebraska wind rises or rain arrives: a shorter interior led route, or a rescheduled exterior on another morning. Weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer, but a venue that has already decided its fallback saves everyone a flustered conversation on the day.

Who should be there, and who should not

The ideal cast is small: your one contact, door holders for the minutes they are needed, and any staff appearing by agreement. Decision makers who want to watch are welcome at the review stage, on site, where the best take is checked before packing away. What a shoot does not need is an audience along the route. Every extra person is a reflection in glass, a shadow in a doorway, or a conversation the schedule bends around.

Children and guests require the strictest version of this rule. If your venue is a school, church, care community, or a business open to the public, preparation includes choosing the window where the route is empty of anyone who has not agreed to be part of the day. That planning is non negotiable, and any operator who treats it lightly should not be filming your space.

The honest limit of one hour

This checklist prepares a space that is already fundamentally ready. It will not fix faded paint in the main room, a broken entrance, or a layout problem you have been meaning to solve, and if you walk the route during this hour and realise the building is not yet the place you want immortalised, the correct move is to postpone the shoot and spend the money after the fix, not before it. We would rather film you once, proud, than twice, apologetic.

Done well, the hour has a side benefit beyond the film. Walking your own building at guest pace, in guest order, shows most venue teams two or three small things they fix for real visitors too. The camera leaves. The clearer entrance stays.
