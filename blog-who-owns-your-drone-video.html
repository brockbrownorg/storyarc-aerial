Who Owns Your Drone Video? Read This Before You Sign Anything

The most common misunderstanding in video buying is simple and expensive: you paid for the film, so you assume you own it outright and can use it anywhere, forever, in any way. In ordinary practice that is not how it works, and the time to learn the difference is before the shoot, not when an ad platform, a sponsor, or a resale of your business raises the question.

December 2025

How it normally works, in plain terms

In ordinary practice, the person or company that creates a video holds the underlying ownership in the footage and the finished edit, unless a written agreement says otherwise. The client receives usage rights, sometimes called a license: permission to use the finished work in stated ways. That arrangement is normal across photography and video. It is not a trick, and it does not make the film any less yours to use for the purposes you bought it for. What matters is whether those purposes are named clearly, in writing, before money changes hands.

Usage is normally granted in terms of where the work may run, how it may run, and for how long. A typical grant for a business film covers your website, your social channels, and your presentations, for the life of the film or for a stated term. Wider uses, such as paid advertising, broadcast, or use by a separate business, a franchise group, or a sponsor, may be included, limited, or priced as a wider grant, because a film running in paid campaigns is doing more commercial work than a film sitting on a homepage. None of this should be improvised after delivery. It belongs in the quote.

Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights together, and our Omaha cost guide explains why usage is one of the factors that moves a price. A film you can use everywhere, for a long term, in paid media, is a broader grant than a film for one channel, and honest pricing reflects that difference.

The four points to get in writing

  • Where it may run. Website, social, paid ads, events, sales presentations, broadcast. Name the channels you actually plan.
  • How long it may run. A stated term, or no end date. If there is a term, know what renewal looks like before the first one expires.
  • Who may use it. Your business only, or also a parent company, a second location, a sponsor, or a partner. A venue in Bellevue and a sister venue in Elkhorn are separate users unless the grant says otherwise.
  • What you receive. The finished film, named cutdowns, and raw files only if the agreement includes them. Raw files are a separate deliverable, not an automatic part of buying a film.

Two more points deserve a sentence each. Music is normally licensed for the finished film as delivered, which means re editing the footage with the same track later, or handing the track to another editor, may fall outside that license. And if you want full ownership transferred rather than usage rights granted, that is a different agreement, usually at a different price, and it must be written explicitly. Silence in a contract does not transfer ownership. This page is plain information about ordinary practice, not legal advice, and a business with a large campaign at stake should have its own advisor read the actual clause.

When the question usually surfaces, too late

Ownership questions rarely arise on delivery day, when everyone is pleased with the film. They arise a year later. You want to put the film into a paid campaign and nobody can say whether ads were included. A sponsor wants to run the event cut on their own channels and their name was never in the grant. You sell the business and the buyer asks what media rights transfer with it. A former partner opens a competing venue and starts using the same film. Each of these is a short conversation before the shoot and a tangled one after it, because the answer depends on words that either exist or do not.

The fix costs nothing. Before you book, write down the uses you can foresee, including the optimistic ones, and ask for the grant to cover them by name. If a use would widen the price, you will learn that at the only moment you can still choose. Here is our cost for saying this plainly: a narrow grant from us costs less, and a buyer whose plans are genuinely narrow should take it and save the money, even though the wider grant pays us more.

What to do with any quote you already hold

Read it once for the total and once for the rights. If it names no usage at all, you do not yet know what you are buying, whatever the film costs. Ask for the clause in writing through the quote page or from your own operator, and do not treat a verbal assurance as a substitute. Words like everywhere and forever should appear on paper, or they do not exist.

Keep the signed quote with the rights clause somewhere you can find it in two years, not only in an inbox thread. The person who needs it then may be you, a new marketing hire, a sponsor contact, or a buyer doing diligence on your business. A short note beside it, naming where the film ran first and what the grant covers, takes five minutes on delivery day and can save a week of reconstruction later. Rights are only useful when they can be found, read, and understood by whoever holds the question, at the moment the question arrives, without calling anyone to interpret them.
