What Event Aerial Coverage Costs: Half Days, Full Days, and What Is Included

You have a date, a budget line, and a board or a boss asking what coverage costs. Event aerial work is not priced by the hour or by the flight. It is priced by the block of the day you buy, and what that block includes matters more than the number.

June 2026

How event coverage is sold

Event coverage is priced by the half day or full day. That framing exists because an event owns its operator for a stretch of an immovable date. Travel, setup, airspace and venue checks, waiting for the right windows, and flying in short planned bursts all sit inside the block, whether the aircraft is in the air for twenty minutes or two hours across it.

A half day suits an event with one clear peak: an opening, a morning of arrivals and gathering, an evening ceremony and sunset. A full day suits a festival or a company day where story builds from setup to close, where sponsor moments are spread across the schedule, or where weather in Nebraska makes a second window later in the day worth owning.

The published figures to plan around are these. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day, and national production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 and up per day. Those are the figures already published on this site for day block work, and they are the honest frame for event coverage budgeting. Your written quote for a specific event may sit inside or around that frame depending on deliverables, and it should say why in plain lines.

What should be included in the block

  • Planning before the day. The location and airspace check, which is free, venue permission coordination, flight windows, and a crowd practice plan.
  • The coverage itself. Aerial filming from clear launch areas, with crowds filmed from the perimeter and at a distance, never by flying over guests, plus ground level work where the quote includes it.
  • A finished recap film, edited with music and colour, ready to post and to send to sponsors.
  • Short cuts for social, named by number and shape in the quote, not promised vaguely.
  • Delivery and rights in writing. Every written quote lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. Finished films within five business days is our stated standard.

If a quote is cheaper because it quietly removes the edit, the social cuts, or the usage rights, it is not the same product at a better price. It is a smaller product. Compare the lists, not only the totals. The fuller breakdown of how our event lane works is on our services page.

What moves an event quote

Deliverables are the biggest lever after time. One master recap costs less than a recap plus a sponsor cut plus a set of vertical clips, because edit hours are real hours. Turnaround is the second. Standard delivery in business days is built into the frame above; a same day or next day edit for a live news cycle will cost more and should be quoted as its own line. Site complexity is the third. A grounds outside Gretna is simpler than a downtown Omaha site where airspace shaped by Eppley Airfield and venue rules need added coordination. Second locations and setup day filming add time and should be named, not absorbed into a hopeful total.

Usage belongs here too. A film for your own channels and sponsor reports is the normal case. Broader paid advertising use, or handing raw files to several partners, changes the value of the work and belongs in the written terms rather than in a later surprise.

What we will talk you out of

Honesty is part of the price guide, not a footnote to it. If your event peak lasts ninety minutes, buy the half day. We will not sell a full day to film waiting. If your event is small, free, and without sponsors or a ticket to sell next year, full coverage may not return its cost at all, and a shorter visit or ground only filming can be the right call. The reason coverage earns at all is laid out in your recap video is next year's ticket sales team: it has to sell, renew, or recruit after the day ends. If none of those jobs exists for your event, the honest price is zero, spent elsewhere.

Weather deserves a money sentence too. Weather rescheduling never punishes the buyer on work that can move, and your agreement for an immovable date should state plainly what happens if the event day cannot fly, which parts convert to ground coverage, and which fees, if any, are affected. Get that in writing before the season, while it is a calm question.

Bring us a date and a programme, and the range we give back will name the block, the deliverables, and the delivery date, so your board is approving a product, not a guess.

A quick way to sanity check any event quote

Read the quote and mark five things with a pen: the block, the deliverables by name, the delivery date, the usage rights, and the weather terms for the fixed date. If all five are present and plain, compare totals with confidence. If one is missing, the price is not yet comparable to a quote that includes it, whatever the number says. This takes ten minutes and it is the same test we invite on our own paper.
