Against the Highlight Reel: In Defense of the Shot Everyone Skips

Scrub through almost any aerial reel and you will feel a strange emptiness after thirty seconds of nonstop spectacle. Everything is amazing, so nothing is. The shots that fix that feeling are the plain, quiet ones most pilots cut first, and I have come to believe cutting them is the central mistake of our craft.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The reel that shouts itself hoarse

I understand the highlight reel because I have made them. Years of FPV flying, freestyle and cinematic, around Omaha and the Midwest produces a hard drive full of peaks. Dives, gaps, reveals, sunsets over the Missouri River. String the peaks together and the result feels powerful for exactly one viewing. On the second viewing a question forms. Where was all of that. Who was it for. What did any of it mean. The reel cannot answer, because the answers lived in the footage that was thrown away.

The thrown away footage is almost always the same kind. The slow approach from a distance. The wide, patient view that lets a place introduce itself. The few seconds of near stillness before the film commits to moving. Pilots call these shots dead air and cut them in the first pass. I did it for years, in my own edits, and I now think that habit cost my early work most of its emotional weight.

So here is the heresy, offered to peers who cut reels for a living. The establishing shot is not the price the audience pays before the film starts. In most aerial work, it is the film, and the spectacle is the garnish. A homepage has about ten seconds to make a visitor feel oriented and curious, a point I develop in my piece on what a homepage film should show first. Orientation is not a lesser artistic goal. It is the precondition for every stronger feeling that follows.

What the quiet shot actually does

An establishing shot performs labor that no amount of speed can perform. It gives the viewer a map. After twenty unhurried seconds above the RiverFront at first light, with the downtown behind and the river bending away toward the bluffs, every later close movement inherits a sense of place. The viewer knows where the camera is and roughly why it matters. Without that map, close movement is just sensation. With it, close movement becomes exploration.

The quiet shot also sets the emotional key. A film that opens in stillness earns the right to move. A film that opens at full throttle has nowhere to go but louder, and louder is a short road. Studying how films are made, I keep returning to directors who hold an ordinary view a few seconds past comfort. They are not wasting time. They are tuning the audience, the way a speaker lowers their voice so a room leans in. My cinewhoop work in quiet interiors taught me the same lesson at small scale. The pause before the movement is where the audience decides to trust you.

There is a final, practical gift. The establishing shot ages well. Trends in speed, transitions, and aggressive color sweep through FPV every couple of years and date everything they touch. A patient wide shot of a real place, honestly framed, looks as true in ten years as it does today. For a business investing in a film it hopes to use for seasons, that durability is not an aesthetic footnote. It is the asset value of the piece.

Where I will still cut it

Honesty requires the other side, and I will give it in writing. The quiet shot can be pure indulgence, and I have cut plenty that deserved cutting. A slow approach that exists because the pilot loved the scenery, rather than because the viewer needed the orientation, is dead air in the true sense. On a short social cut built for a feed, where the contract with the viewer is measured in seconds, leading with thirty quiet seconds can be a genuine mistake. Context decides. My argument is not that every film needs a long establishing shot. It is that the shot deserves a trial before its execution, and in most edits it never gets one.

I apply a plain test now, learned from editing my own work and flinching at the results. If removing the quiet shot makes the film harder to follow or easier to forget, the shot stays, whatever it does to the pace on paper. If removing it changes nothing, it goes, whatever it does to my feelings. Pilots who cannot pass that test honestly are not defending art. They are defending footage, which is a different and lesser loyalty.

The wider services question follows naturally. A film built only from peaks may win a week of attention and lose the year. The work described on our services page is built for the year: a homepage film that orients, a flythrough that testifies, a recap that remembers the whole day and not only its loudest minute. Peaks belong in those films. They just cannot be all that is in them.

A modest proposal to my fellow pilots

Keep your best establishing shot in your next reel. Not sped up, not buried under a transition, not apologized for. Let it sit at the front at its honest length and watch how the response changes. Producers will read confidence in it. Viewers will settle into the film instead of bracing against it. And you may feel what I felt the first time I tried it, a small embarrassment at how many good films I had weakened by skipping the shot that made them make sense.

The highlight reel promises that excitement is a pile of exciting moments. It is not. Excitement is a structure, with ground to stand on and a reason to leap. The shot everyone skips is the ground. Defend it, and the leaps you keep will finally land.
