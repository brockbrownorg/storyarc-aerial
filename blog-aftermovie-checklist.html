The Aftermovie Checklist: Deliverables to Agree Before the Gates Open

After the event, everyone wants the film at once: sponsors, your board, your social feed, next year's ticket page. If the deliverables were never agreed in writing, that first week becomes a negotiation you do not have time for.

June 2026

Agree the films themselves, by name

An aftermovie is rarely one film. It is a small family of cuts, and each one should be named before the day so filming can serve all of them.

  • The master recap. Length, tone, and where it will live: homepage, ticket page, annual meeting.
  • Social cuts. How many, which shapes, vertical for phones and square or wide where needed, and whether captions are included.
  • The sponsor cut. One shared film or custom cuts per major backer, and which sponsor moments must appear.
  • A teaser for next year, if dates exist, cut while excitement is fresh rather than rebuilt months later.
  • Raw or clean location footage, if your team or a partner will edit later. Say so now; file handling differs.

If a deliverable is not on this list, assume it is not included. That single assumption prevents most after event disappointment, and it costs nothing to test while planning is still on paper.

Agree formats, delivery, and rights

Every written quote should list deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights. For events, expand that standard into the details your team will actually need.

  • Delivery date for each cut. Finished films within five business days is our standard for the main film. If a sponsor cut or a same week teaser runs on a different clock, write that clock down.
  • File formats and sizes for your website, your social accounts, and any screen at next year's venue.
  • Music and usage rights. Where the film may run, for how long, whether paid advertising is included, and whether sponsors may post their cut on their own channels.
  • Revisions. How many rounds are included, who gives one consolidated set of notes, and how fast changes turn around.
  • Archive. How long the project files are kept, and what a re edit next year would cost.

Agree the day itself, because deliverables depend on it

A checklist that only covers files will still fail if the filming day was never specified. Write down flight windows, launch and landing areas away from guests, and the crowd rule in plain words: crowds are filmed from the perimeter and at a distance, and there is no flying over guests. Write down who approves a change on the day, and the weather decision points, because a Nebraska storm or a rise in wind can move a peak shot to its fallback.

Include site facts while you are at it. Venue permission, any airspace need for a site shaped by Eppley Airfield near downtown Omaha or the RiverFront, parking and access for the crew, and a run sheet contact who will actually answer on the day. The location and airspace check is free, and its findings belong in this document, not in a separate thread nobody can find at gates open.

The honesty item most checklists miss

Add one line most organizers skip: what will not be filmed, and what happens if the day cannot fly. If weather closes the air entirely, your film becomes a ground level film, and the checklist should say whether the deliverables, the price block, and the delivery date change. Naming that now is not pessimism. It is what lets you inform sponsors calmly instead of apologising later.

There is a budget honesty too. Every extra cut is edit time. If the list outgrows the block you bought, something has to give: fewer social cuts, a later delivery for the archive material, or a larger budget approved before the event. Deciding that tradeoff in advance is your job and your operator's job together. The value that justifies the list at all is explained in your recap video is next year's ticket sales team. Each deliverable should map to a use: a sale, a renewal, a report, a launch. A cut with no use is the first one to release when the day gets hard.

Walk into gates open with this checklist signed, and the week after your event is for posting, thanking, and selling. Walk in without it, and the same week is for chasing files. The event is identical. The preparation is the difference.

Who owns the checklist inside your team

Give the checklist one owner on your side, usually the organiser or a single deputy, and one owner on the crew side. Sponsor requests, board requests, and social requests all flow to that one person, who keeps the agreed list as the single source of truth. Without that discipline, a well meaning volunteer can promise a sponsor an extra cut at the gate, a board member can ask the crew for a new film on the day, and the edit week opens with three versions of what was agreed. One page, two owners, and a rule that changes get written onto the page will prevent nearly all of it.

Keep the signed checklist with your event files after the day as well. Next year planning starts from what actually worked: which cuts were used, which sponsor asked for more, which delivery date felt tight. That record is worth as much as the film when a new board or a new sponsor lead takes over.
