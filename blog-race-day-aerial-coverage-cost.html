What Race Day Aerial Coverage Costs: Day Rates, Weekends, and Seasons

You are trying to budget aerial coverage for a race day and every operator answers a different way, some with a day rate, some with a weekend figure, some with a season pitch, and none of them explaining how the numbers relate. They do relate, and once you see the blocks, you can budget any of them.

July 2026

The building block is the event day

Action and motorsports coverage is priced by the event day. That is the structure stated on our cost guide and the structure this page will use throughout. A day is not an hour count with a drone in the air. It is a planned day of work around your program: the permission and briefing work before flying, the coverage itself across the sessions that matter, the ground material that supports it, and the files and films that come out of it, scoped in a written quote.

For the closest published comparison, look at production day blocks. Story led operators in this region publish $850 for a half day and $1,650 for a full day. National production support commonly runs between $1,500 and $2,000 and up per day. A race day sits in that production family rather than in the hobby family, because the buyer is purchasing a planned, permitted, deliverable producing day at an organized event, and those published blocks are the honest frame for budgeting one.

One condition shapes every figure on this page and should shape your budget too. We cover sanctioned events and closed courses with organizer permission only. The permission work is part of what a day rate buys, not an extra, and how that permission is actually earned at a race track explains why a legitimate day costs what it does. A quote that leaves permission to you has not saved you money. It has handed you the hardest part of the job.

What moves a single day up or down

  • Deliverables. Raw files for your own editor cost less than a finished film with vertical cuts. Our production files arrive within two business days and finished films within five business days, and the quote states which you are buying.
  • Turnaround. Same day vertical cuts add editing work inside the day itself. Standard delivery on a business day clock costs less than a clock that ends before the pits clear.
  • Length and shape of the program. A short evening program and a full day program with multiple classes are both one date on a calendar and very different amounts of work. The quote should describe the program it assumes.
  • Locations in the day. One venue is the base case. A day split across sites, or a series round that moves, adds travel and planning that belong in the figure.
  • Usage. Where the films will run and for how long belongs in writing. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and broader usage is worth more than a single post because it works longer.

Our action and motorsports coverage is scoped this way on purpose, so a promoter comparing two quotes can compare the days being described rather than the totals alone. If another operator quotes far below the production blocks above, ask what the day leaves out. It will be something, and it is usually the permission, the edit, or the usage rights.

Weekends and seasons are built, not discounted into existence

A weekend is usually two event days with shared planning. The venue, the permission, the crew, and the rhythm are already in place on the second day, so a weekend figure is built by describing both days honestly rather than by doubling or halving a single rate on instinct. A championship weekend with longer programs and more deliverables will price above a regular weekend at the same track, and the quote should say why in plain language.

A season is a set of event days agreed in advance, with season level deliverables added: a championship film, sponsor season material, and a promo for the following year. The honest way to budget a season is to price the rounds you actually need covered, at the deliverable level you actually need, and then add the season pieces as named items. Beware any season pitch that starts from a large undifferentiated total and then looks for rounds to justify it. The rounds are the product. The total is arithmetic.

There is also a timing honesty to seasons. Locking dates early gives a crew the ability to plan around your calendar and gives you a document you can sell sponsorship against. It does not entitle either side to pretend every round is identical. A season quote that names each venue and each program shape is worth more than a flat promise, because the first time a round runs long or moves, the named quote survives and the flat promise becomes an argument.

The other tier of this market, named honestly

There is a second, entirely different market at most tracks, and naming it may save you from comparing unlike things. At the grassroots level, individual riders and drivers buy short clips of themselves, priced per rider and per class, from media sellers working the event that day. It is a high volume, low price trade, it serves casual demand well, and it is not what StoryArc sells. We sell planned day, weekend, and season coverage to organizers, series, teams, and racers building a program. If per rider clips are what your budget allows, that tier is the honest buy, and we would rather name it than stretch a day rate down into a shape that would fail you.

So the budgeting answer, in one place: plan around a production day, expect the regional full day block of $1,650 to be the reference point serious operators in this region publish, build weekends from the actual days involved, and build seasons from the actual rounds. Then get the figure for your event in writing, with deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights listed, and compare every other quote against that document line by line.
