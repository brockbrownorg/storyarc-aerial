Recruiting Films That Show the Workplace From Above

A candidate in Omaha is rarely choosing between you and nothing. They are choosing between you and the employer across town who made the job feel real first. A recruiting film wins when a good candidate can picture their own day at your place before they ever apply.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Candidates are buying a day, not a job title

Job posts all sound alike. Duties, requirements, benefits, a paragraph about culture that could belong to anyone. What a candidate cannot get from a post is the feeling of the place: how big it is, where they would park and walk in, who they would work beside, and whether the building and the grounds say this is a serious and cared for operation.

That is the gap a recruiting film fills. An opening view of the whole workplace, a campus in Elkhorn, a plant in Bellevue, a yard or a hospital or a distribution site on the edge of the metro, gives a candidate instant orientation. Then ground level filming does the part aerial cannot: the team, the training, the break room, the small signs that people are treated well here. Place gets attention. People get applications.

  • The arrival. Where a new hire parks, enters, and is greeted on a first morning.
  • The team. Real employees at real work, in the roles you are hiring for.
  • The path. Training, mentoring, and where a person in this role can be in a year.
  • The place in its town. The commute, the neighborhood, the metro a relocating candidate is weighing.

What aerial adds to a recruiting film

Use the air for orientation and pride. A single move across the full site tells a candidate this is a substantial and stable employer, and for multi building workplaces it explains in seconds what a map and a paragraph fumble through. Seasonal timing matters in Nebraska: a campus filmed in green summer light reads very differently from the same place in February, and an honest plan either picks the season that matches most of the working year or shows the place plainly and lets the people carry the warmth.

Keep aerial in its place, though. Candidates do not choose an employer because a roof looked good from above. They choose because a supervisor sounded like someone they could learn from and a coworker described a day that sounded like a day they want. That is why the planning on our services page treats brand and recruiting films as story first, with aerial as the opening and the context rather than the whole message.

Practical details matter more than polish here. Film the actual shift the role works where you can. Show the actual equipment the role uses. If the job is physical, let it look physical. A film that hides the hard parts produces applicants who leave in the first month, which is the most expensive hiring outcome of all.

Build it once, use it across the hiring year

A recruiting shoot planned as a library will serve far beyond one opening. Follow the approach in the homepage film guide and think in layers: one main film for the careers page, short cuts for individual roles, a version for job fairs and school visits, and quiet looping footage for a lobby or a booth. Agree all of that before filming day, in a written quote that lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights.

Update honestly as you go. If a team changes or a site expands, a short return visit keeps the film true. A recruiting film that shows people who left two years ago and a building you have since outgrown quietly teaches candidates to discount everything else you say.

The honest limits

A film cannot outrun a workplace people are leaving for reasons you already know. If pay, scheduling, or a difficult supervisor is driving turnover, a recruiting film will bring in candidates who discover the same facts and leave, and it can make the problem more expensive. Fix the cause first. Film the fix when it is real, and let employees describe the change in their own words.

It is also worth saying that a small team hiring one person a year rarely needs a produced film. A sincere phone video from the hiring manager, naming the role and showing the desk and the team, can do that job for almost nothing. Save the produced film for repeated hiring, hard to fill roles, or a workplace whose scale and setting are genuinely part of why a candidate should choose you. Spent there, it earns. Spent as decoration, it does not.

Measure the film by the quality of applicants, not by views alone. Ask new hires where they first saw the workplace, note which roles drew stronger candidates after the film appeared, and let that plain record guide the next version. A recruiting film that shortens time spent filling hard roles and improves the fit of people who apply has paid for itself in a currency any hiring manager understands.

Share the finished film with the employees who appear in it before it goes public, and let them share it onward themselves. A recruiting message carried by the people in it travels further and sounds truer than the same message carried by the company alone.
