Aerial for Agriculture: Fields, Facilities, and the Story of Scale

Nobody understands your operation from a photo taken at the fence. The fields, the bins, the yards, the facilities, and the way they fit together across a section of Nebraska ground: that is the business, and it only reads whole from the air.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

Scale is the story in agriculture

In most industries, aerial film is a choice. In agriculture, it is close to a necessity, because the asset itself is land. A buyer weighing a seed or feed supplier, a family weighing an operation to partner with, a lender or a landlord forming a judgment, all of them are asking the same plain questions. How much ground? In what condition? Run by people who keep a place up or let it slide?

A slow pass at the right season answers in seconds. Green fields in clean lines. Facilities kept in order. Grain storage, livestock facilities, machine sheds, and yards arranged by people who plan. From the ground, that is twenty separate photos and a lot of explaining. From the air, it is one honest view.

  • The land. Fields in season, boundaries clear, the operation seen as a whole.
  • The facilities. Bins, barns, shops, and yards, filmed clean and in good light.
  • The work. Planting, spraying windows, harvest, and feeding, where timing and permission allow.
  • The place. The operation in its county and community, near the towns it serves.

Timing is everything, and Nebraska sets it

An agricultural film lives or dies on the calendar. The same field is bare dirt in April, a green sea in July, gold in September, and stubble under a low sun in November. Decide what story the business needs before choosing a date. A supplier selling reliability may want facilities and summer fields at their fullest. An operation telling a family story may want harvest, when several generations and several machines are in the same frame and the year of work becomes visible.

Wind is the other Nebraska fact. Open ground offers no shelter, and a calm morning is worth more than a convenient afternoon. Build the plan around early and late light and accept that weather rescheduling is part of honest aerial work in this state. With us it never punishes the buyer, and for seasonal filming that promise belongs in the conversation at the start, not after a windy day.

Permission and courtesy matter on rural ground too. Neighbors notice an aircraft they were not expecting. A plan that tells adjoining landowners what is happening and when, keeps flights over the client ground, and respects livestock that could be unsettled by a low pass is simply good practice, and it protects the relationships an ag business lives on.

Where the film does its work

Put the finished film where decisions form. On the homepage, following the pattern in the homepage film guide, opening on the land and then coming down to the people. In conversations with buyers and partners who will never drive out to see the place before they form an opinion. At trade shows and county and state fair settings, where a moving view of Nebraska ground stops people in a way a banner cannot. And inside the family, where a film of the operation in a good year becomes a record the next generation keeps.

Our services page describes brand story work built for exactly this kind of business, where the setting is not decoration but the substance of the offer. Plan the shoot as a library as well: the main film, short seasonal cuts, and still useful clips of individual facilities, so one well timed day serves the website, social, and sales conversations across the year.

Agree the deliverables in writing before anyone flies. Every written quote from us lists deliverables, delivery date, and usage rights, and finished films arrive within five business days, which matters when the film is meant to catch a season or a show date.

The honest limits

Aerial film will not make a small operation look large to anyone who knows the country, and it should not try. Neighbors and buyers can read ground truth at a glance, and a film that strains to impress will be read as exactly that. Film the operation at its true size, kept the way you actually keep it, and let steadiness be the selling point. In agriculture, that is usually the point worth making.

There are also jobs where a flight adds nothing. A single product, a repair service, or a retail counter in town is a ground level story of people and shelves, not fields. And some seasons should simply be skipped: mud, dormancy, and a yard in midwinter disorder will not help a business that looks strong in June. Wait for the season that tells the truth in your favor. Patience is part of the craft out here, and any operator who has farmed around Nebraska weather already knows it.

Consider also what the film means inside the operation itself. Crews and family members rarely see the whole place at once, and a true view of it in a good season builds a shared pride that carries into how the work is described to neighbors and customers. That internal value is hard to price, and anyone who has watched a team see its own ground properly for the first time will not dismiss it.
