Same Day Vertical Cuts: Why the Pits Clear With Content Already Posted

By the time you have loaded the trailer, the conversation about your race day is already over online, and the teams who posted during the event are the ones in it. That gap is not about budget. It is about whether the day was planned for content from the start.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The race is only half the deadline

A race result has a short life. The evening of the event, people who were there are posting, people who were not there are curious, and sponsors and series accounts are looking for something worth sharing. By the middle of the following week the same footage is history, still useful, but no longer news. Teams who understand that plan two products from one day: the short vertical cuts that travel the same day, and the finished film that arrives later and carries the lasting value.

This matters in the Midwest in a particular way. Much of the racing within reach of Omaha runs on weekly or monthly programs, at places like Eagle Raceway and LSF MX Park in Lincoln, or the drag and road course programs at I-29 Speedway in Pacific Junction. When your next round is seven days away, last weekend's content is this weekend's entry promotion. Posting late does not just waste attention, it wastes the next gate.

To be clear about what same day means, because the phrase gets abused: same day cuts are short selects, not the finished film. A finished film from us arrives within five business days, with proper color, sound, and structure. Anyone who promises you a finished film the same evening is either cutting every corner or redefining the word finished, and you will see which when you open the file.

What has to be true before the day starts

Same day delivery is decided before the first engine starts. It needs a shot plan built around short vertical moments rather than around a long edit: the arrival, the grid, one or two passes that show the place and the field, the pits working, and the finish or podium. It needs an editing rhythm agreed in advance, with the phone or laptop work happening in the gaps of the program, not in a heroic session after the final.

  • Permission settled early. We fly sanctioned events and closed courses with organizer permission only, and same day pressure never changes that. If approval is still open on race morning, the aerial part of the plan waits.
  • A short list of named moments. Three to five clips the team actually wants, agreed before the day, so nobody is reviewing an hour of footage in a parking lot.
  • Captions and tags ready. The names, series, sponsors, and accounts written down in advance, because typing them from memory at midnight is how sponsors get misspelled.
  • A realistic ceiling. A handful of strong cuts posted well beats a flood of rough clips posted fast, and a sponsor can tell the difference at a glance.

Our action and motorsports coverage is priced by the event day, and the same day cuts are part of how that day is scoped in the written quote, with deliverables and delivery dates listed. If same day posting matters to you, it belongs in the quote, not in a hopeful conversation on the way home.

How the day actually runs

The work starts before the racing does. Arrival and setup footage is often the most shared material of the day, because it is the part everyone who stayed home wishes they could see. During the program, flying happens in the windows the schedule and the officials allow, briefed with race control and stopped whenever the day says stop. Between sessions, the selects are pulled and the vertical cuts are built, so posting happens across the afternoon and evening rather than in one late dump.

The ground carries a share of this that aerial alone cannot. Pit work, faces, and the small moments between races are what make a cut feel like being there, and they are also the safest and most reliable material on a windy Nebraska day. When the wind rises, and on open ground in this state it will, the plan shifts weight to the ground and the aerial list shortens. Saying that in advance is part of the service. A crew that promises every planned flight regardless of weather is promising something it does not control.

The approval side of all this is not paperwork for its own sake. It is what lets a crew be on site, briefed, and trusted enough to work quickly. How an approved crew gets permission at a race track explains that process, and it is the reason same day work is possible at all: nobody moves fast at an event where they are not supposed to be.

When same day is not worth paying for

Here is the honest part. If your team posts rarely, has no sponsor obligations, and mainly wants one good film for the end of the season, same day cuts add cost and pressure for attention you will not use. Take the finished film, post it well, and spend the difference elsewhere. Same day earns its keep when there is an audience waiting that evening: a series building the next round, a sponsor expecting visibility while the event is live, or a track selling the next gate. Name which of those is true for you before you buy the faster clock.

It is also not worth it when the day itself is wrong for it. A first event with a new organizer, where permission is fresh and the program is untested, is a day to cover carefully and post the following morning, not a day to race the internet. Judgment about when to move fast is part of what a day rate buys, and a crew willing to slow down on the right day is worth more than one that never does.
