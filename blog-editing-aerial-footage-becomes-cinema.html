Editing Is Where Aerial Footage Becomes Cinema

Every pilot I know can describe their best flight. Far fewer can describe their best cut, and that imbalance explains most of the forgettable drone footage on the internet. The flight is only the harvest. Cinema happens later, in the edit, or it does not happen at all.

By Brock Brown, founder and pilot, StoryArc Aerial · November 2026

The harvest is not the meal

I edit my own work, and I have done so for as long as I have flown cameras around Omaha and the Midwest. That single fact has shaped my flying more than any aircraft I own. When you know you will personally sit with every second of a flight, in an edit bay, at night, with the freedom to be brutally honest, you fly differently. You stop collecting spectacle. You start collecting decisions an editor can use.

The distinction matters to buyers more than they realize. I explore what clients actually receive in my guide to raw footage versus a finished film, but the philosophical point belongs here. Raw aerial footage is a pile of possibilities, some of them contradictory. One flight argues the place is serene. The next argues it is thrilling. Both cannot lead the same film. Editing is the act of deciding which argument the place is actually making, and every cut after that either serves the decision or betrays it.

My claim, and editors are welcome to test it, is this. A pilot who will not edit is only doing half the craft, and the missing half is the half the audience experiences. Nobody outside our small world remembers flights. Audiences remember sequences. A sequence is an edited thing, built from intention, rhythm, and omission. Pilots who treat editing as a chore to outsource are carpenters who refuse to enter the house they framed.

What the timeline teaches the sticks

The edit bay is a merciless flying instructor. It shows you, frame by frame, where your speed stopped serving the shot. It shows you the half second of searching before a turn that you swore was clean. It shows you reveal after reveal arriving too early, because in the air every reveal feels earned and on the timeline most of them feel rushed. I keep a private list of lessons the timeline has taught me, and every item on it changed a habit in the air.

Hold longer than feels natural, because an editor can trim a hold and cannot invent one. Enter a movement already decided, because the audience reads hesitation as confusion even in beautiful light. Fly an ending for every shot, a place where the movement resolves, because a clip that simply stops gives the editor nothing to cut away from with grace. Leave quiet footage around the loud moments, because contrast is what makes loud read as loud. None of these lessons came from a flight manual. All of them came from sitting in DaVinci Resolve with my own work and nowhere to hide.

Editing also teaches proportion. A freestyle pilot in an open Nebraska field experiences a four minute flight as a single rising arc. An editor knows the audience will grant about twenty seconds of any one idea before they need the film to turn. Learning to think in twenty second truths while flying four minute lines is a strange double vision, and it is the core skill of cinematic FPV. The flight stays whole in the hands. In the mind, it is already being cut.

Deletion is the real craft

Ask what makes an aerial film feel cinematic and people reach for color, music, and lenses. In my experience it is deletion. The cinematic quality of a sequence is mostly the quality of what was removed. Weak middles. Repeated ideas. The gorgeous shot that belongs to a different film. I have cut flights I crossed counties to capture because the finished piece was stronger without them, and I expect to keep doing exactly that.

Here is the honesty this essay owes. A large share of what I fly deserves the delete key, and any pilot who claims otherwise is either a genius or a poor editor. I would rather print that than pretend every battery produces gold. The willingness to discard is what separates a filmmaker from a footage collector, and it is a discipline the audience never sees and always feels. They feel it as pace. They feel it as confidence. They feel it as the sense that the film respects their time.

That respect is also why I take the handover to other editors seriously, in the spirit of our page for filmmakers. When another editor will cut my flying, I owe them footage that arrives with clean entries, clean exits, honest variety, and no vanity sequences wasting their morning. Editing my own work taught me what a gift that is, because I have been the editor cursing the pilot at midnight. The pilot was me. The lesson stuck.

The flight serves the film

None of this diminishes flying. The edit cannot invent a movement that never happened, and a timid flight gives an editor timid options. But the hierarchy matters and I will defend it against any pilot who wants the takeoff to be the creative peak. The flight serves the sequence. The sequence serves the film. The film serves the viewer. Work backward from the viewer and every decision sharpens. Work forward from the aircraft and you get a reel of beautiful answers to questions nobody asked.

So yes, I love the flying, the cinewhoops threading a quiet interior, the freestyle craft climbing into a Nebraska evening. I love it enough to subject it to the timeline without mercy. Editing is where aerial footage becomes cinema, not because editing is magic, but because editing is judgment, and judgment is the one ingredient no aircraft ships with.
